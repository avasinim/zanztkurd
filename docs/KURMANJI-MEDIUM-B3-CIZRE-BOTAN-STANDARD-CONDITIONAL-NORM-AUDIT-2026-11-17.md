## 1. Executive finding

A defensible policy for Pathway B is to use **Standard Kurmanjî in Hawar-based Roman orthography** as the written reference norm, with the grammar described by Haig and Öpengin as the principal descriptive frame. That standard is described as loosely based on Cizre/Botan, but it is not a direct transcription of one local dialect: Haig explicitly says it mixes forms from other dialects and is therefore an artificial norm in the ordinary sense of language standardization.

For conditional clauses, the current evidence supports a more cautious statement:

- Teach the Standard Kurmanjî present subjunctive as the normal form in real/possible condition protases, with a future-marked consequence as the principal NK pattern.
- Teach past counterfactuals as a paired construction: a conditional/past-conditional form in the protasis and a separately selected past-imperfect or future-perfect form in the apodosis, depending on the semantic category and the descriptive reference grammar.
- Do **not** present one past-counterfactual apodosis pattern as the only Kurmanji pattern. The comparative corpus directly records more than one configuration.
- Keep the Thackston past-perfect-conditional paradigms as a reference-grammar description, not as proof that every Cizîr/Botan speaker uses those exact variants.
- Do not import Bahdini future-perfect `dê (bi)zanî-ye` or its distinct habitual/conditional particle `dā` into the default Standard Kurmanjî paradigm.

This is a recommendation for a future editorial decision, not approval to write or change course lessons.

## 2. What “Cizîr/Botan-oriented standard” actually means

Haig’s chapter “Northern Kurdish (Kurmanjî)” makes three distinctions that should control our terminology:

1. The grammatical sketch labelled “Standard Kurmanjî” is loosely based on Cizre and Botan in southeastern Turkey.
2. The written standard is broadly accepted and uses a modified Roman alphabet, with norms substantially based on Celadet Ali Bedir Khan’s *Hawar* conventions and later codification in Bedir-Khan and Lescot.
3. The standard is not a pure rendering of Cizre/ Botan speech; it incorporates forms from other dialects and is an artificial norm in the technical sense of standardization.

Therefore the course label should be **“Standard Kurmanjî (Hawar-based; Cizîr/Botan-oriented)”**, not “pure Cizîr dialect” and not “all Kurmanji dialects.”

The chapter also treats Behdinî of northern Iraq separately, and describes the Kurmanji speech area as a dialect continuum with meaningful regional variation. The southeastern varieties, including Behdinî and Şemzînan, retain richer morphology than many varieties farther northwest.

## 3. What the comparative conditional corpus can and cannot establish

Matras’s comparative chapter draws on the Manchester Database of Kurdish Dialects (MDKD): a free-speech corpus of about 50,000 words from 71 locations, with 44 NK/Kurmanji-Bahdini and 25 CK samples. It also uses elicited conditional sentences. The author warns that the questionnaire does not comprehensively cover all complex clauses, although the selected material is suitable for examining conditional clause formation.

Crucially, the published conditional examples identify their sample locations. The unreal-condition examples include Uludere (Turkey), Kamyaran (Iran), Kelhê (Syria), and Khalakan (Iraq); the intermediate-potential examples include Tatvan (Turkey), Mosul (Iraq), Khalakan (Iraq), and Oshnaviyeh (Iran). The examples located in southeastern Turkey are not identified as Cizre itself. The corpus therefore supports regional comparison, but it does **not** independently establish the exact conditional paradigm of a speaker specifically from Cizre or Botan.

This is an important evidence boundary: “Standard Kurmanjî is loosely based on Cizre/Botan” does not mean “the MDKD conditional samples are Cizre/Botan samples.”

## 4. Real/possible conditions: recommended teaching baseline

Haig’s Standard Kurmanjî sketch presents the present subjunctive as a core mood for irrealis and subordinate clauses, including conditional examples introduced by `heke`. Matras’s comparative elicitation reports the present subjunctive in the condition clause across the sampled real-condition varieties. In the consequence clause, most NK samples use future tense; five NK samples use present indicative, while CK samples use present indicative.

**Recommended default for the Standard Kurmanjî pathway:** teach the present subjunctive in the protasis and a future-marked consequence as the principal NK written pattern. Add a short usage note that present-indicative consequences are also attested in a minority of NK samples and are common in the CK samples; do not present CK as the Kurmanji default.

Do not overstate the data as “every Kurmanji dialect must use future in the apodosis.” Matras’s quantified result explicitly allows exceptions.

## 5. Unreal/past counterfactuals: what the sources say

### 5.1 Thackston reference grammar

Thackston §25.2 describes two past-perfect-conditional formations and says they appear mutually exclusive by dialect: one uses the past perfect plus `-(y)a`; the other uses subjunctive `bi-/ne-` and `ba-` in place of `bû`. The grammar describes the past-perfect conditional in the protasis and either past-perfect conditional or future-perfect conditional in the apodosis, with special rules for clauses containing `bûn` or `karîn`.

These are detailed paradigmatic data, but the grammar itself marks the two patterns as dialect-conditioned. They must not be collapsed into one synthetic paradigm or taught as though every speaker alternates freely between them.

### 5.2 Matras comparative evidence

For the elicited fully unreal condition “If I had known that you would not come to the wedding, I would not have gone there either,” Matras reports:
- Uludere, Turkey: `zanî-ba` in the condition clause, with a past-imperfect consequence;
- Kamyaran, Iran: `zanî-ba-m` in the condition clause, with a past-imperfect consequence;
- Kelhê, Syria: `bi-zani-ya` in the condition clause, with a past-imperfect consequence;
- Khalakan, Iraq: `bi-m-zany-aye` in the condition clause, with a past-imperfect consequence.

A number of CK and SK samples from Iran use the imperfect in both clauses. Matras suggests Persian influence as a possible explanation, not a proven causal account.

The comparative evidence thus confirms that conditional morphology and consequence-clause tense-aspect are regionally variable. It does not justify replacing the Thackston paradigms with a single universal form.

### 5.3 Intermediate potential conditions

Matras separately identifies a middle category: the event is unlikely but not impossible. In the examples, most samples use a conditional or past-conditional form in the condition clause. NK samples use a future-perfect consequence, while CK samples use an imperfect consequence. Matras therefore treats this category as separately represented in the sampled NK data but not in the sampled CK data.

**Pedagogical consequence:** do not teach this intermediate category as if it were merely another name for fully unreal past counterfactuals. It deserves its own semantic explanation and clause-pair pattern.

## 6. Bahdini boundary: do not merge it into the default

Matras records a Bahdini future-perfect shape `dê (bi)zanî-ye` alongside other NK future-perfect forms. Haig’s discussion of Behdinî also identifies features that distinguish it from Standard Kurmanjî and other NK varieties. A separate chapter on the Iranian languages of northern Iraq reports a Behdinî past-habitual form built with `dā`, also used in a conditional sense, plus the non-prefixed present stem; the author notes that this feature is unique to Behdinî within NK and that its conditional/irrealis use is also discussed in Unger (2014).

Accordingly:
- do not use `dê (bi)zanî-ye` as the default Standard Kurmanjî future-perfect model;
- do not teach `dā` as a general Kurmanji conditional marker;
- if Bahdini is later included, label it as a separate regional variety and validate its paradigm with a specialist source and native editor.

## 7. Recommended pathway policy

The following is the strongest policy supported by the sources, pending owner approval:

| Layer | Recommended policy | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| Written orthography | Hawar-based modified Roman orthography | Broadly accepted written standard described by Haig |
| Main grammar label | Standard Kurmanjî, Cizîr/Botan-oriented | Descriptive standard, not a pure local dialect |
| Real-condition protasis | Present subjunctive | Strong comparative support |
| Real-condition consequence | Future-marked form as default NK pattern | Majority NK pattern; exceptions documented |
| Fully unreal protasis | A past-conditional construction from the chosen Standard K. reference grammar | Reference-grammar support; regional variants documented |
| Fully unreal consequence | Select according to the semantic construction and chosen Standard K. grammar; explicitly flag comparative past-imperfect alternatives | Variation documented; not one universal form |
| Intermediate potential condition | Separate semantic category; do not merge with fully unreal | Comparative corpus support |
| Bahdini alternatives | Separate labelled note or future separate track | Distinctive regional forms documented |

### Editorial safeguards

1. Name the standard precisely; do not claim it is a pure dialect.
2. Choose one paradigm per lesson and use it consistently; do not mix Thackston’s two mutually exclusive past-perfect-conditional variants.
3. Distinguish real, fully unreal, and intermediate-potential conditions by meaning before presenting morphology.
4. Teach the protasis and apodosis as a system, not as an isolated verb tense.
5. Mark regional alternatives by location and source, rather than calling them “mistakes.”
6. Keep Sorani/Central Kurdish and Bahdini examples out of the main Kurmanji-only sequence unless the lesson is explicitly comparative.
7. Have a qualified Cizîr/Botan-oriented Kurmanji editor review the exact forms, spelling, and acceptability before any learner-facing publication.

## 8. Evidence limitations and open questions

- Haig’s 2018 chapter defines the Standard Kurmanjî reference norm but does not give a full dedicated paradigm for every conditional construction.
- Matras’s comparative corpus includes many regions but does not identify a Cizre sample for the conditional examples discussed here.
- Thackston gives detailed conditional paradigms but explicitly says the two past-perfect conditional formations are dialect-conditioned.
- The MDKD questionnaire’s complex-clause coverage is not comprehensive.
- No source consulted here establishes a single obligatory apodosis pattern for all Cizîr/Botan speakers in every counterfactual context.
- Native-editor verification remains necessary before learner-facing use.
