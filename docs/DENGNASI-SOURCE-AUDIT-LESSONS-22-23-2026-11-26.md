## Lesson 22 — coverage and targeted omissions

The authored lesson follows the source's main sequence and preserves its central argument without endorsing either alphabet as inherently superior:

- KL and KE naming, historical Cyrillic/Armenian use, and the point that neither standard alphabet is restricted to one Kurdish variety (pp. 348–349).
- The 31-letter KL inventory, historical spelling variants, KE's 33/34-sign count, and the major differences in writing direction, letter joining, letter shapes, and vowel notation (pp. 348–350).
- Shared origins and the source's cautions about representing every phonetic distinction in ordinary spelling (pp. 350–351).
- The distinctions among **hevdeng**, **hevnivîs**, **hevwate**, **hevreh**, and **cêwî/cêmik**, including stress-based homographs and examples of historical doublets (pp. 352–359).
- The Kurdish–Persian sound correspondences and the source's qualifications for A, B/V/W, D/Z, E/I, Ë/I, G, H, M/V, O, Ş/H, Û/O, V/W and X (pp. 359–363).
- The discussion of proposed extra letters for Ç/K/P/T, R, L and H, and the author's conclusion that phonetic detail belongs in dictionaries and pronunciation guides rather than forcing every variant into ordinary orthography (pp. 363–372).

A closer comparison found two concrete details that are not explicit in the current authored lesson and should be restored in a subsequent content-sync pass:

1. **The L section is too compressed.** The source gives the marginal Kurmanji cases *welat*, *sal* (especially *wê salê*, *sala 2013*), and some dialectal *milet* and *mela*. It also explicitly discusses adding **ł** to KL or doubling L as alternatives, before arguing that one L better serves shared spelling (printed page 369). The authored lesson states the general conclusion but omits these examples and the alternatives.
2. **The spelling-versus-pronunciation illustration is shortened.** The source's discussion on printed page 371 explains that a reader can use context to distinguish *ker* “donkey” from *ker* “deaf”, and suggests *kerr* as an optional disambiguating spelling. This concrete example is not present in the current lesson. It is useful to the source's argument that ordinary orthography need not encode every phonetic detail.

These are source-supported omissions, not evidence that the entire lesson is inaccurate. They have been recorded for restoration; no new linguistic examples have been invented.

## Lesson 23 — coverage verified in this targeted pass

The authored lesson represents the source's major sections and examples:

- The three types of dialect difference (lexical, morphological, phonological), methodological scope, and explicit non-hierarchical treatment of Kurdish varieties (pp. 373–376).
- Kurmanji V versus Sorani W/M, including *çav/çaw*, *nav/naw*, *sêv/sêw*, *şev/şew*, *dev/dem* and *ev/em* (p. 377).
- Sorani RD versus Kurmanji R/RT, including *birdin/birin*, *kirdin/kirin*, *mirdin/mirin*, *pird/pir*, *sard/sar*, *xwardin/xwarin*, and the exception *bijardin/bijartin* (p. 378).
- Initial ST and Sorani prothesis/deletion patterns, including the listed exceptions and borrowed forms (p. 379).
- Aspirated/unaspirated Ç/K/P/T, emphatic consonants, and H correspondences and exceptions (pp. 379–381).
- Kurmanji I versus Sorani E, Î versus Ê, verb suffixes, light/dark L, Û/UH versus WÊ, XWE versus XO, and G/K palatalization before Ê/WÊ (pp. 382–386).

The source's own internal inconsistency in the K palatalization discussion is retained as a flagged source issue: the prose generalizes a [ç] outcome before Ê/WÊ, while its *kî/kê → kê* example is explicitly transcribed [kê], not [çê] (printed page 386). The authored lesson correctly flags rather than silently “correcting” this discrepancy.
