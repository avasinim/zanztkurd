# Kurmanji bûn Constructions — Copula, Inchoative, Complex Predicate and Regional Passive Claims Audit — 2026-11-17

## 1. Purpose and status

This source-critical audit separates four constructions that must not be collapsed merely because they contain the verb **bûn**:

1. copular **bûn** ‘be’ (stative predication);
2. inchoative **bûn** ‘become’ (change of state);
3. adjective-/noun-/particle-plus-verb complex predicates (lexical predicate formation);
4. the noun/adjective + **bûn** passive strategy reported by Mizban (2026) for Kurmanji in northeastern and northern Iran.

The first three are independently documented in descriptive and syntactic work on Kurmanji. The fourth is a recent, regionally bounded claim whose full chapter, examples and method could not be inspected in the public preview. It is therefore recorded as a research hypothesis about a particular contact setting, not adopted as a general Kurmanji passive rule.

Documentation and research only. No lesson text, course inventory, seed, code, schema, migration, CI, database, production system, PR, deployment or main branch is changed.

## 3. Copular bûn versus inchoative bûn

A single English gloss ‘be’ conceals a grammatical contrast. The descriptive account by Gündoğdu Yücel (2018), in its discussion of the copula, distinguishes:

- **stative/copular** bûn: a subject is characterized or identified by a predicate (‘be’);
- **inchoative** bûn: the subject enters or comes to have a state (‘become’).

The dissertation's examples include **Ez xwandekar im** ‘I am a student’ as a stative copular clause, and an inchoative use glossed ‘become clever / become human’. The source explicitly notes a difference in the placement of the predicative complement: the stative complement is preverbal, while the inchoative complement is postverbal in the described construction.

Pedagogical consequence:
- annotate the sense as COP ‘be’ or INCH ‘become’ rather than assigning one undifferentiated lexical gloss;
- record complement position and inflection separately;
- do not treat an adjective followed by bûn as automatically passive: it may be an ordinary inchoative (‘become X’).

This is a descriptive generalization from the cited Kurmanji grammar, not a claim that every regional variety has identical surface details.

## 4. Complex predicates: lexical category is not enough

Haig (2002, §§4 and 6) classifies Kurmanji complex predicates by both the category of the non-verbal element and the transitivity/structure of the verbal element. His Table 1 includes:
- adjective + intransitive verb: **rast hatin** ‘encounter’;
- adjective + transitive verb: **pîroz kirin** ‘celebrate’;
- noun + intransitive verb: **dilketin** ‘fall in love’;
- particle + verb: **derketin** ‘go out’, **vekirin** ‘open’.

Haig argues that the boundaries between noun, adjective and particle may be blurred in complex-predicate position. His discussion of **çêkirin / çêbûn** (originally an adjective-like element, now largely restricted to those combinations) illustrates why a purely dictionary-category label may not predict the construction's syntax. He also proposes a continuum of fusion between the non-verbal element and the verb.

The analysis must therefore be stored per predicate and sense:
- lexicalized meaning and attested variety/register;
- whether the non-verbal element remains an independent argument, is incorporated, or is structurally inert;
- argument frame and case/agreement behavior;
- whether the construction has a corresponding inchoative/intransitive or causative/transitive counterpart.

Do not infer that every N/ADJ + bûn sequence is a passive. It may be an inchoative, a lexicalized predicate, or a result/state expression.

## 5. What the established Kurmanji passive diagnostics show

Haig (2002, §4, pp. 24–25 in the article pagination) describes the Kurmanji passive as a form of **hatin** ‘come’ plus the infinitive of the main verb. A direct object, and only a direct object in the diagnostic discussed there, advances to subjecthood; the original agent is deleted in the canonical pattern. He illustrates passive forms of complex predicates including **avdan**, **naskirin**, and **dermankirin**.

This is important for the current audit for two reasons:

1. A complex predicate can itself be passivized while its non-verbal component remains fused with the verb. The promoted subject is not necessarily that non-verbal component.
2. The mere fact that a compound predicate contains **bûn** does not demonstrate that it has passive voice. The analysis needs evidence of an active counterpart, argument mapping/patient promotion, and agent suppression or demotion.

The earlier project audit already records **hatin + infinitive** as the established core passive strategy for the target Kurmanji pathway. This document does not withdraw or replace that baseline.

## 6. Mizban (2026): what the abstract does and does not establish

Bibliographic record:
- Elham Mizban, “Passive Structure in Kurmānji and Its Morphological and Sociological Changes,” in *Caspian Languages and Linguistics* (Springer, 2026), pp. 201–225, DOI: 10.1007/978-981-95-1390-1_9.
- Publicly accessible material inspected: the abstract and metadata page, not the full chapter.

The abstract states:
- 1,258 sentences were extracted from radio programs, interviews and semi-structured questions;
- 100 were excluded as incomplete or irrelevant; 1,158 were categorized and analyzed;
- the target is Kurmanji in northeastern and northern areas of Iran;
- the historical construction is described as **hatin** plus infinitive/noun/adjective and as continuing to passivize simple verbs;
- a second construction is described as noun/adjective + **bûn**, used to passivize compound verbs;
- the abstract reports 36.45% for the historical construction and 63.54% for the newer analytic construction;
- it associates the newer pattern with Persian structural alignment and reports correlations with education and a slight gender difference.

These are claims reported by the chapter abstract. They cannot yet be independently checked because the public preview did not expose the chapter's examples, coding scheme, tables, denominator definitions, speaker distribution or full analysis. The percentages sum to 99.99% because of rounding; this is not itself a problem, but the exact denominator and category assignment still need inspection.

### Required verification before any generalization

The full chapter or author-supplied text must be examined for:
1. exact N/ADJ + bûn examples, transcription, glosses and translations;
2. whether the non-verbal element is a noun, adjective, participle, resultative predicate or lexicalized complex-predicate component;
3. an identifiable active transitive counterpart for each passive candidate;
4. evidence that the active patient/object becomes the passive subject;
5. whether an agent phrase is possible, optional, absent, or categorically excluded;
6. whether the construction is eventive passive, resultative adjective, inchoative/change-of-state, or lexicalized compound;
7. source distribution (radio, interview, elicitation), speaker counts, locality, age, education and gender;
8. whether the 36.45/63.54 percentages are tokens, types, speakers, or another denominator;
9. the exact basis for attributing the construction to Persian alignment, rather than parallel innovation, lexicalization, register or other contact effects;
10. whether independent Kurmanji varieties outside the Iranian sample show the same structure.

No example should be invented to fill these gaps.

## 7. Regional and sociolinguistic limits

Mizban's abstract explicitly limits its empirical target to northeastern/northern Iranian Kurmanji. Its results cannot automatically be transferred to Muş, Botan, Cizîr, Amed, Serhed, Turkey-wide standard written Kurmanji, Syria, Iraq, the diaspora, or the Academy's chosen instructional standard.

The abstract's reported association with education and gender is correlational. It does not establish that education or gender causes the construction, nor does it license a normative judgment that the construction is incorrect or inferior.

The Persian-contact interpretation is a hypothesis to test against the chapter's examples and independent comparative data. Do not label the construction “Persianized” as an established fact until the evidence and the proposed structural correspondence are examined.

## 8. Editorial and course policy

For the Kurmanji-medium pathway:
- retain **hatin + infinitive** as the established passive baseline;
- teach the distinction between copular ‘be’ and inchoative ‘become’ separately;
- treat adjective-/noun-plus-light-verb forms as lexical complex predicates unless their syntax establishes a different analysis;
- keep Mizban's N/ADJ + bûn passive claim in a clearly marked regional research note only;
- do not present it as a productive, universal, or standard Kurmanji passive rule;
- do not add examples, change lesson wording, or update seed/course files without explicit approval;
- preserve variety labels and source provenance on every example.

## 10. Conclusion

The safe grammatical distinction is not “bûn = passive”. Kurmanji **bûn** has a stative copular use and an inchoative ‘become’ use; it also participates in lexical complex predicates. Haig's independently inspected Kurmanji evidence documents a canonical **hatin + infinitive** passive and shows that complex predicates can be passivized without their non-verbal component becoming the promoted subject. Mizban (2026) reports a newer N/ADJ + bûn passive in a defined Iranian Kurmanji sample, but the abstract alone does not permit a general standard-Kurmanji rule or a fully verified syntactic analysis.

**Decision:** preserve the existing instructional baseline; quarantine the newer construction as regionally bounded, abstract-level evidence pending full-text verification and native-speaker review.
