# Kurmanji dan 'Give' — Light-Verb Predicates, Recipient Structure and Analytic Causatives Audit — 2026-11-17

## 1. Purpose and scope

This audit separates four constructions that can look deceptively similar because they contain the verb **dan**:

1. ordinary lexical **dan** ‘give’, with a recipient/goal;
2. lexicalized noun + **dan** complex predicates (N+dan LVCs);
3. analytic causatives in which **dan** functions as a causative auxiliary;
4. compounds or preverb + **dan** verbs whose meanings have become lexicalized.

It also records the word-order variation reported for recipients and goals, and distinguishes a recipient clitic from the homophonous-looking directional clitic discussed in Kurmanji descriptions.

The central editorial warning is that a string containing **dan** does not by itself tell us whether it is a lexical verb, light verb, auxiliary or component of a lexicalized compound. Classification must be based on constructional syntax and meaning, not orthographic spacing or English gloss alone.

## 2. The lexical verb dan 'give'

The ordinary lexical verb **dan** has a transfer meaning: an agent gives or transfers something to a recipient. The clause can therefore involve at least:
- a giver/agent;
- a transferred theme;
- a recipient or goal.

Haig's Kurmanji grammatical sketch describes neutral clause order as broadly SOVG, where G includes goals of motion, recipients of transfer and addressees of speech. It also notes that recipients of **dan** are generally placed immediately after the predicate in an oblique form, without an adposition, in the dialects surveyed. Badini and Şemzînan are noted as varieties that frequently use **bo** with such post-predicate recipients/goals. Other dialects may use the circumposition **ji ... ra** for recipient/benefactive relations, with a different order.

This variation is important for teaching: the recipient frame cannot be reduced to one invariant preposition or one invariant position across all Kurmanji varieties.

A recipient clitic must also be distinguished from the directional clitic. Haig notes forms such as **didin=ê** ‘they give to him/her’ and **got=ê** ‘said to him/her’; where this recipient clitic is present, the directional particle cannot also be realized. Do not label every verbal =e/=ê ending as a directional marker without checking its function.

## 3. Noun + dan light-verb constructions

Kurmanji uses **dan** as a light verb with nominal elements. Attested examples in Gündoğdu (2016) include:
- **av dan** ‘water’ → ‘irrigate / water’;
- **bal dan** ‘attention give’ → ‘pay attention’;
- **gire dan** ‘knot give’ → ‘tie’;
- **xeber dan** ‘information give’ → ‘speak / tell’.

Akin & Bouveret (2021) also report N+dan LVCs in their corpus, including:
- **piştgirî dan** ‘support’;
- **zirar dan** ‘damage’;
- **agahî dan** ‘inform’;
- **ders dan** ‘teach / give a lesson’;
- **rûmet dan** ‘show respect’.

These are lexical complex predicates. The nominal element carries much of the predicate's lexical content, while **dan** contributes a comparatively light verbal component. But their argument frames are not automatically identical to the ordinary three-participant transfer frame of lexical ‘give’.

Editorially, do not translate every N+dan construction word-for-word as ‘give X’. Record its established lexical meaning, the participants it licenses and its attested variety/register. Do not assume that every noun can freely combine with **dan** to form a conventional predicate.

## 4. Compound and preverb + dan forms

Akin & Bouveret distinguish lexicalized word + **dan** compounds from LVCs and analytic causatives. Examples reported in their discussion include:
- **rûdan** ‘happen’;
- **soz dan** ‘promise’;
- **nîşan dan** ‘show’;
- **hewldan** ‘strive / make an effort’.

They also list preverb/prefix combinations such as:
- **hildan** ‘lift / raise’;
- **vedan** ‘separate / dig / return’ (meanings vary by lexical entry);
- **berdan** ‘let go / abandon’.

The forms above must be treated as lexical entries with their own inflection and senses. Their spelling as one word in a source is evidence of that source's orthographic treatment, not proof that every N+dan LVC must be joined orthographically. Conversely, a space in another source does not establish that the two elements are syntactically independent in every context.

## 5. Analytic causatives with dan

Akin & Bouveret (2021) focus on the grammaticalization of **dan** from lexical ‘give’ toward a causative auxiliary. They distinguish three broad causative patterns.

### 5.1 Simple causative: dan + infinitive

The construction **dan + V-Inf** expresses causing someone to perform an action or bringing about an event. The paper identifies this as the most frequent causative pattern in its corpus, after the LVC category.

An example given is **dan nasîn** ‘make known / teach’, with a clause meaning ‘we teach them / make them know ...’. This is not an N+dan light-verb predicate: **dan** precedes a separate infinitive and functions as a causative operator.

The construction may encode a causer and a causee in addition to the participant structure of the infinitive. Exact case marking and order must be taken from the attested clause and the relevant variety; they should not be reconstructed from an English paraphrase.

### 5.2 Double causation: dan + causative-marked verb

The paper also discusses **dan + V-and**, in which the lexical verb carries a causative marker in addition to the causative contribution of **dan**. This is described as double causation.

The two causative contributions must not be collapsed into a simple lexical translation. A record should specify:
- which verb bears the morphological causative marking;
- which participant causes the event;
- which participant directly causes or performs the embedded event;
- whether the two causative layers are both semantically active in the example.

The existence of the pattern does not authorize adding **-and** to arbitrary verbs. The morphological distribution and lexical restrictions require verb-by-verb evidence.

### 5.3 Complex causative: dan + kirin + infinitive

Akin & Bouveret further describe **dan + kirin V-Inf** as a complex causative construction. This is distinct from a simple N+dan LVC and from **dan + V-Inf**.

The internal **kirin** element belongs to the causative construction described by the authors. Do not misparse it as the ordinary lexical N+kirin pattern merely because it is adjacent to a verbal element. The clause structure and infinitive are decisive.

### 5.4 Causative force and interpretation

The source discusses different strengths/interpretations of causation, including permissive or ‘let’ readings and stronger/directive causation, depending on the construction and context. The exact force is not a one-to-one fixed translation of **dan**:
- lexical ‘give’ is transfer;
- an analytic causative may mean cause, make, have, let or order, depending on construction and context;
- a lexicalized compound may have a meaning not compositionally predictable from ‘give’.

Therefore, **dan** should not be assigned one invariant gloss across all these structures.

## 6. Keep the four construction types apart

| Construction | Diagnostic shape | Function | Editorial classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lexical transfer | finite **dan** with theme and recipient | give / transfer | simplex lexical verb |
| N+dan LVC | nominal element + **dan** | lexical predicate such as ‘support’, ‘teach’, ‘pay attention’ | complex predicate |
| Analytic causative | **dan + V-Inf** | cause/let someone do something | auxiliary construction |
| Complex/double causative | **dan + kirin + V-Inf** or **dan + V-and** | layered causation | causative construction |

This table is a diagnostic summary, not a claim that all forms have identical syntax in every dialect or register.

## 7. Valency and argument realization

The four construction types differ in their participant structure.

### Lexical transfer

The lexical verb 'give' typically has a giver, a theme and a recipient/goal. The recipient's case and position vary by dialect. Do not treat the recipient as a direct object without testing the relevant case/agreement behavior.

### N+dan LVC

The nominal component and light verb form a lexical predicate. The predicate may take an object or another complement, but the frame is construction-specific. For example, ‘teach’ and ‘support’ need not share the same argument structure simply because both contain **dan**.

### Analytic causative

The causative construction adds a causer to the event expressed by the infinitive and makes available a causee/embedded agent. The case and word order of these participants must be checked in the exact construction; a simple transfer frame must not be copied over.

### Double causative

The outer causative and the morphological causative marking can encode more than one causal layer. Do not infer the number of syntactic arguments from the number of causative markers alone; inspect the clause and the intended event structure.

## 8. A source-aware lexical and grammar record template

For every candidate with **dan**, record:

1. exact attested form and orthography;
2. source, page/example, date and corpus/register;
3. variety/locality (e.g. standard written Kurmanjî, Muş, Badini, Şemzînan);
4. classification: lexical transfer / N+dan LVC / lexicalized compound / analytic causative;
5. lexical meaning and literal compositional gloss, kept in separate fields;
6. finite or non-finite status of **dan**;
7. whether a following infinitive is present;
8. whether **kirin** is part of the causative construction;
9. whether the lexical verb bears morphological causative **-and**;
10. causer, causee, theme, recipient and other complement roles;
11. case, agreement and word order in the attested clause;
12. whether the meaning is permissive, directive, indirect or direct causation;
13. whether the form is productive or lexicalized, with evidence;
14. orthographic joining/separation as a source-specific convention.

Do not create a paradigm or sample sentence when the source only gives a dictionary-style list or an isolated example.

## 9. Teaching decisions for the Kurmanji-medium pathway

- Introduce lexical **dan** ‘give’ with its transfer frame and dialect-sensitive recipient placement.
- Teach selected N+dan LVCs as lexical vocabulary items, not as a fully productive ‘noun + give’ rule.
- Teach analytic causative **dan + infinitive** separately from N+dan LVCs.
- Introduce **dan + kirin + infinitive** and **dan + V-and** only after the learner has a clear distinction between infinitive, finite verb and morphological causative.
- Keep permissive ‘let’ and strong/directive ‘make/order’ readings contextual; do not teach them as automatic translations of **dan**.
- Do not teach a universal recipient preposition or fixed postverbal order: Haig reports dialectal differences, including **bo** in Badini and Şemzînan.
- Distinguish recipient clitics from directional clitics.
- Do not transfer Sorani forms or case rules into the Kurmanji pathway.
- Do not alter existing lessons or seed data without explicit approval.

## 11. Conclusion

**dan** spans lexical transfer, noun + light-verb predicates, lexicalized compounds and analytic causative constructions. These categories differ in finiteness, argument structure and semantic contribution. Akin & Bouveret's corpus study distinguishes three causative patterns—**dan + infinitive**, **dan + kirin + infinitive**, and **dan + causative-marked verb**—while Haig documents dialect-sensitive recipient placement and the need to distinguish recipient clitics from directional marking.

**Decision:** store and teach each **dan** construction under its own structural class and attested lexical frame. Do not use a single generic ‘dan = give’ rule, do not conflate LVCs with causatives, and do not generalize one dialect's recipient order to the whole Kurmanji standard.
