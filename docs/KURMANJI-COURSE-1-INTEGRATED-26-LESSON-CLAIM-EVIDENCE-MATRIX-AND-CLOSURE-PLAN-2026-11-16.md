Evidence terms in this synthesis:
- **Supported:** the central linguistic proposition is corroborated by an appropriate source at the stated scope.
- **Partial:** the broad point is supported, but one or more examples, glosses, dialect limits, or technical details remain unchecked.
- **Open:** a material claim/example is not yet resolved or requires a specialist/editor decision.
- **Editorial:** language quality, Sorani translation, terminology, pedagogy, or house style needs a qualified editor; this is not settled by Kurmanji grammar sources.

A general rule being supported does not authenticate every sentence used to teach it. A source example must not be represented as a quotation unless the wording and provenance match.

## 4. Cross-course linguistic consistency map

### 4.1 Grapheme, phoneme, pronunciation and spelling

Lessons 01–08 and 26 must use four distinct concepts:
1. **Grapheme/letter:** an element of the adopted writing system.
2. **Phoneme:** a contrastive sound category in the linguistic analysis.
3. **Phonetic realization:** a pronunciation, potentially variable by context or dialect.
4. **Orthographic convention:** the way a sound or contrast is represented in writing.

Haig (2018), “Northern Kurdish (Kurmanjî),” discusses word-initial trilled `r`, variable tap/trill distribution elsewhere, and cross-dialect phonological variation. This supports a phonetic distinction but does not by itself establish that `rr` is a separate alphabet letter or that every trill must be written with a doubled letter. The 31-letter Hawar inventory must be kept distinct from the phoneme inventory.

**Do not** silently add a 32nd letter, alter `roj`, or create a minimal pair based on the internally inconsistent Arizona `rr/roj` table row.

### 4.2 Gender, ezafe, nominal morphology and case

Lessons 11–12 and 21–25 form one dependency chain. Haig (2018) describes two grammatical genders in nouns, gender reflected in ezafe and singular Oblique marking, neutralization of gender distinctions in plural forms, the Direct/Oblique opposition, and the indefinite suffix `-ek`. These are interdependent categories; lesson explanations must not teach them as unrelated suffix lists.

The course must distinguish:
- gender/number agreement on ezafe;
- case marking on noun phrases and pronouns;
- indefinite suffix `-ek`;
- free or demonstrative ezafe forms;
- direct/oblique distribution by clause tense/aspect and dialect.

Avoid categorical statements that a suffix or paradigm applies uniformly across every Kurmanji variety.

### 4.3 Verbal morphology, negation, questions and word order

Lessons 17–20 and 25 should share one consistent framework:
- present indicative `di-` patterns are not a universal template for irregular and compound verbs;
- negative `na-` patterns require exception handling and separate treatment of copular/existential/imperative constructions;
- interrogative form and case (notably `kî/kê`) must be taught together where relevant;
- SOV is a neutral/default tendency, not a rule that excludes focus/fronting or goal/recipient placement;
- present and past transitive alignment must be explained with Direct/Oblique terminology, not reduced to subject/object labels.

### 4.4 Dialect scope

Haig & Öpengin (2018) and Haig (2018) describe substantial regional variation. The course owner/editor has not yet selected the spoken reference variety. Therefore:
- the Hawar-based Latin written target can be stated as a course policy proposal;
- spoken forms, morphology and pronunciation that vary regionally must be labelled;
- no regional variant should be silently marked wrong;
- examples should carry provenance and, where known, locality/variety labels.

1. Select the intended spoken/reference variety for instruction, including what counts as a standard pedagogical form.

3. Decide how to teach `R/RR`: letter inventory, phonetic realization, and spelling convention must remain separate.
4. Select the canonical content source when migration and split-seed lesson payloads diverge; do not choose by recency alone or overwrite one with the other.
5. Confirm whether variants belong in the student lesson, teacher notes, or an advanced supplement.

### P0 — Kurmanji specialist validation

1. Recheck every exact inflected sentence flagged in the five detailed registers, not only its underlying grammar rule.
2. Verify pronoun, demonstrative, nominal gender/case and ezafe paradigms against the selected reference variety.
3. Verify present-tense, negation, interrogative, comparative and possession examples.
4. Verify all pronunciation/IPA tables and the treatment of `r/rr`.
5. Mark every example as **attested**, **adapted**, or **instructor-created**, with traceable provenance.

### P1 — native Sorani editorial pass

Review all Sorani explanations, glosses, punctuation, spelling and translations across lessons 01–26. This must be a dedicated editorial pass; Kurmanji grammar sources do not certify Sorani writing quality. No global substitutions should be made before the house style is approved.

### P1 — technical orthography reference

For claims about Unicode normalization, sorting, collation and storage of diacritics, cite the relevant Unicode/locale technical standard. Linguistic grammars are not sufficient evidence for software sorting behavior.

### P2 — pedagogy and assessment

1. Maintain a glossary of technical terms and use one definition consistently.
2. Ensure each lesson's examples actually demonstrate the rule being taught.
3. Keep dialect variants clearly labelled rather than mixed in one paradigm.
4. Treat lesson 26 checklist as formative self-assessment, not a validated proficiency instrument.
5. After corrections are approved, create a traceability table linking each learning outcome to explanation, examples, exercise, feedback and assessment item.
