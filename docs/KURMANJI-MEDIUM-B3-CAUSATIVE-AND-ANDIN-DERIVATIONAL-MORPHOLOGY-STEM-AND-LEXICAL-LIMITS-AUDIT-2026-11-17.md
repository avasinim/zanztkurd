# Kurmanji Causative -and/-andin — Derivational Morphology, Stem Selection and Lexical Limits Audit — 2026-11-17

## 1. Purpose and boundary

This audit examines the Kurmanji causative derivation commonly described as **-and**, with infinitives often ending in **-andin**. It distinguishes the derivational operation from the ordinary infinitive ending, from the present/past stem system, and from the separate periphrastic causative auxiliary **dan**.

This is a research and editorial document only. It does not authorize changes to lessons, seed data, application code, CI, migrations, database contents, production systems or deployment.

## 2. Core generalization in the descriptive grammar

Haig (2018, §2.6, pp. 124–125) characterizes the causative suffix **-and** as the only moderately productive derivational process for creating new verbs in Kurmanji. It derives transitive verbs from intransitive present stems. The examples are:
- **gerîn** ‘walk, stroll’ → **gerandin** ‘lead’;
- **nivistin** ‘sleep’ → **nivandin** ‘put to sleep’.

The wording “moderately productive” is important. It does not license mechanically adding -and to every intransitive verb. Haig explicitly presents the verb class as small and relatively closed, and says the relation between present and past stems is not fully predictable.

Akin & Bouveret's 2021 chapter independently discusses **-and** in intransitive/transitive pairs:
- **firîn** ‘fly’ → **firandin** ‘make fly’;
- **meşîn** ‘run’ → **meşandin** ‘make run’;
- **gerîn** ‘walk’ → **gerandin** ‘make walk’.

Their analysis describes the derived member as transitive and causative, and treats the alternation as tied to the verb's capacity to be transitivized.

## 3. Do not conflate -and, -andin and -în

Three different descriptive levels must be kept separate:

1. **Derivational marker:** Haig calls the causative suffix **-and**.
2. **Infinitive form:** derived verbs are cited with infinitives such as **gerandin**, **nivandin**, **firandin**. A separate lexicographic description says the causative can be formed by adding the infinitive ending **-andin** to the present stem of many intransitives, with a present stem in **-ên**.
3. **Finite stems:** Kurmanji verbs have present and past stems, and the stem relation is not predictable in all verbs. The derived verb must therefore receive its own recorded present and past stem evidence; neither should be guessed from the infinitive.

The surface sequence **-andin** should not be treated as proof that the entire sequence is a single invariant suffix in every morphological analysis. The precise segmentation and phonological realization need to be tied to the source's framework and the individual verb.

## 5. Semantic relation: causation and valency

The derivation typically adds an external causer and increases the verb's valency:
- base intransitive: an entity undergoes or performs the event (e.g. someone sleeps);
- derived causative/transitive: an external causer brings about the event (e.g. someone puts another person to sleep).

However, the English gloss is only a guide. The derived verb may lexicalize a more specific meaning such as ‘lead’, rather than a transparent ‘make walk’. A lexical entry must preserve both:
- the transparent derivational relation, if supported;
- the conventional lexical meaning of the derived verb.

For each pair, annotate:
- base predicate's argument structure;
- derived predicate's argument structure;
- causer/agent;
- causee/embedded participant;
- any theme or additional object;
- case marking in present and past;
- whether the derived meaning remains compositionally predictable.

Do not infer direct vs indirect causation from the suffix alone. The derivation marks a causative/transitive relation; the degree of control, intention, immediacy or permissiveness is contextual and lexical.

## 6. Stem and paradigm implications

Haig's description requires special care:
- the language has distinct present and past stems;
- their relationship is not fully predictable;
- the suffix is described as deriving transitive verbs from intransitive present stems;
- derived forms are lexical verbs and therefore need their own finite stem record.

A teaching paradigm must not simply attach the infinitive form **-andin** to a guessed base stem and then invent all finite forms. For every verb:
1. verify the infinitive in a reputable dictionary or grammar;
2. locate the present stem of the base;
3. verify the derived verb's present stem;
4. verify the derived verb's past stem;
5. check whether the derivation preserves or changes the stem vowel/consonants;
6. check actual person endings and prefix placement in the target standard/variety;
7. verify the derived verb's transitivity and case frame from attested clauses.

Haig's Table 12 shows why a general base-stem algorithm is unsafe: common Kurmanji verbs exhibit suppletion and stem alternations, and the author declines to offer a fully predictable classification.

## 7. Keep morphological causatives distinct from periphrastic dan

The following structures are related semantically but must remain distinct records:

### A. Morphological causative
**gerîn → gerandin**

A single derived lexical verb; the derived verb has its own inflectional paradigm.

### B. Periphrastic causative
**dan + infinitive**

A finite causative auxiliary plus a lexical infinitive. The finite auxiliary carries TAM/person inflection; the embedded verb remains infinitival in the cited construction.

### C. Double causative
**dan + V-and**

A periphrastic causative applied to an already morphologically causativized verb. Akin & Bouveret cite **dan nasandin** as a double causative and describe **dan rûniştandin** as an extended form where the lexicalized causative verb already exists.

The double form is attested, but it must not be treated as mandatory or stylistically neutral in every variety. The authors explicitly discuss cases where adding **dan** may be redundant or reflect extension of the productive construction.

## 8. Lexicalization and false positives

Not every verb whose spelling contains a sequence resembling **-and** is necessarily a synchronically productive causative. The editor must distinguish:
- an attested base/derived intransitive-transitive pair;
- a historically derived but synchronically lexicalized verb;
- a verb with a coincidental sequence;
- a loan or dialect form;
- a transcription/orthographic variant.

A candidate pair should be accepted as a causative derivation only when supported by at least:
1. a credible lexical/grammatical source for both forms;
2. evidence that the base is intransitive and the derived form transitive;
3. compatible meaning and/or an explicit causative analysis;
4. source variety and orthographic form.

The dictionary note that sound-describing verbs are often causative in form is another warning against deriving a universal rule from surface shape alone.

## 9. Passive morphology is separate

A lexicographic description of Kurmanji states that passive forms are normally built with **-ran** (infinitive ending) and present stem **-rê** from transitive or causative verbs, with irregular passives separately listed. This is a separate voice operation, not a second causative suffix. Any future treatment of passive forms must be audited independently, including irregular forms and variety-specific patterns.

Do not conflate:
- causative derivation **-and**;
- infinitive citation forms **-andin**;
- passive derivation **-ran**;
- periphrastic causative auxiliary **dan**.

## 10. Recommended lexical database fields

For each base/derived pair, preserve:
- base infinitive, present stem and past stem;
- derived infinitive, present stem and past stem;
- source spelling and normalized Hawar spelling in separate fields;
- IPA/phonetic evidence where available;
- transitivity of each member;
- argument frames and case marking;
- derivational segmentation and the source's terminology;
- lexical meaning and compositional gloss;
- variety/locality/register;
- source citation and page/example;
- evidence grade (primary attestation, grammar description, dictionary, corpus);
- native-editor review status;
- confidence and unresolved questions.

A pair-level relation record should link the two lexical entries; do not collapse both words into one entry.

## 11. Quality gates before lesson use

1. No productive paradigm from suffix spelling alone.
2. No invented causative pairs.
3. Confirm base and derived transitivity with actual examples.
4. Record derived present and past stems separately.
5. Separate lexicalized meanings from transparent causation.
6. Do not generalize Haig's Standard Kurmanji description to all regional varieties without evidence.
7. Preserve the difference between **-and** morphology and auxiliary **dan**.
8. Check spelling and segmentation in the original source page, especially for forms where the source transcription varies.
9. Obtain native-editor review before presenting an example as a model form.
