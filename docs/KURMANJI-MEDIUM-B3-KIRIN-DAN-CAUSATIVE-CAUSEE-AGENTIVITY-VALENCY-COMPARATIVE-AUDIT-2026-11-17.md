# Kurmanji Causative Constructions — kirin vs dan, Causee Realization, Agentivity and Valency Audit — 2026-11-17

## 1. Scope and research question

This audit compares **kirin** and **dan** in causative constructions in Kurmanji. The purpose is not to assign a single English equivalent to either verb, but to distinguish:
- lexical **kirin** ‘do/make’ and lexicalized N+kirin predicates;
- **kirin** as a light verb in lexical causative predicates;
- analytic **dan + V-Inf** causatives;
- analytic **dan + kirin + V-Inf** complex causatives;
- morphological causatives in **-and** and their possible co-occurrence with **dan**;
- ordinary transitive clauses that express causation without either auxiliary.

The key finding is that **kirin** and **dan** are not freely interchangeable causative markers. They belong to different constructional patterns and contribute different degrees or kinds of lexical and grammatical meaning. The cited literature also warns that the causative **dan** pattern is not equally available across all Kurdish dialects.

This is a documentation-only research note. No lessons, course inventory, source lesson text, code, seed, schema, migrations, CI, database, production, PR, deployment or main branch are changed.

## 2. Evidence base and dialect boundary

Akin & Bouveret (2021) analyze the use and grammaticalization of **dan** in Kurmanji, using dictionary, newspaper and corpus data. Their chapter distinguishes light-verb constructions from analytic causatives and identifies three causative patterns:
1. **dan + V-Inf**;
2. **dan + V-and** (analytic plus morphological causative);
3. **dan + kirin + V-Inf**.

The authors describe the causative force of **dan** as extending from permissive/weak causation (‘let’) toward stronger manipulative or directive causation (‘make/order’), depending on the construction and context.

Their discussion also reports a dialect-survey limitation: a translation prompt equivalent to ‘He caused Ahmed to get arrested’ did not elicit a **dan** causative in that survey, and the authors note the absence of this causative pattern in the Sorani data they discuss. Therefore, the findings here concern Kurmanji evidence; they must not be transferred to Sorani or assumed to be universal across all Kurmanji varieties.

## 3. kirin: lexical verb, light verb and causative resource

### 3.1 Lexical kirin

The verb **kirin** has ordinary lexical uses such as ‘do’ and ‘make’. A clause with a lexical transitive verb can describe an agent causing a result without being an analytic causative construction. For example, a transitive verb may directly encode breaking, moving, standing someone up or changing an object's state. The presence of an agent and an affected participant is not enough to classify a clause as a grammatical causative.

### 3.2 N + kirin light-verb predicates

In N+V complex predicates, **kirin** combines with a nominal element to form a lexical predicate. Examples discussed in Kurmanji research include:
- **bawer kirin** ‘believe’;
- **fêm kirin** ‘understand’;
- **qebûl kirin** ‘accept’;
- **gazî kirin** ‘call’;
- **mêze kirin** ‘look at’ (with a selected PP in Haig's analysis);
- **şerm kirin** ‘be ashamed’.

These are not all causatives. Their lexical meanings and argument structures differ. In particular, **qebûl kirin** ‘accept’ is not automatically an analytic causative simply because an English paraphrase could contain ‘make acceptable’. The construction's syntax, not the translation, determines its class.

### 3.3 kirin as a causative light verb inside a larger construction

Akin & Bouveret describe **dan + kirin + V-Inf** as a complex causative. Within this pattern, **kirin** contributes a causative/light-verb layer associated with the embedded infinitive, while **dan** functions as an outer semi-auxiliary of causation. The authors analyze the combination as a double/complex causative, not as an ordinary N+kirin LVC.

Their examples include:
- **dan çapkirin** ‘make print / publish’;
- **dan guftûgokirin** ‘make negotiate’;
- **dan qebûlkirin** ‘make accept’.

The authors describe this construction as agentive and associated with external causation in their corpus. That characterization is source-specific; it should not be expanded into an unrestricted rule for every lexical verb.

## 4. dan: analytic causative and degrees of causal force

### 4.1 Simple analytic causative: dan + infinitive

The construction **dan + V-Inf** is the most frequent causative pattern in Akin & Bouveret's corpus. It is analyzed as a semi-auxiliary construction: **dan** has moved beyond full lexical transfer ‘give’ toward grammatical causation.

An example is **dan nasîn** ‘make known / teach’. Here the infinitive is a separate verbal element. This is structurally different from an N+dan LVC such as **av dan** ‘irrigate’ or **bal dan** ‘pay attention’.

### 4.2 Causal force is contextual

Akin & Bouveret distinguish different types of causation, including:
- internal direct causation;
- external direct causation;
- indirect causation;
- implicit causation;
- manipulative causation.

They also discuss a force/strength continuum in which **dan** may be interpreted closer to ‘let’ at the weaker/permissive end and closer to ‘order’ at the stronger/directive end. This does not mean every **dan + infinitive** token has one fixed reading. The causal relationship and degree of control must be inferred from the whole clause and context.

### 4.3 dan + V-and: layered causation

The authors discuss **dan + V-and**, where the lexical verb bears a morphological causative marker in addition to the causative contribution of **dan**. Their examples contrast intransitive forms such as **firîn** ‘fly’, **meşîn** ‘run’ and **gerîn** ‘walk’ with causative forms **firandin**, **meşandin** and **gerandin** (‘make fly/run/walk’).

A further **dan** may occur with such a causative form, creating a layered or double causative. The paper gives a form equivalent to ‘make [someone] sit/stand someone up’ in which a lexicalized causative is already available and **dan** is added. The authors interpret this as extension of the analytic causative construction.

Editorial caution: the source explicitly notes that the etymological relation between **-and** and **dan** is not established. Do not teach **-and** as derived from **dan**. Do not add the marker productively to arbitrary stems.

## 5. kirin vs dan: constructional comparison

| Diagnostic | kirin | dan |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary lexical meaning | do / make | give / transfer |
| N + light verb | common lexical N+kirin predicates | common lexical N+dan predicates |
| Analytic causative pattern in Akin & Bouveret | participates as a causative light verb within **dan + kirin + V-Inf** | outer causative semi-auxiliary in **dan + V-Inf** |
| Morphological causative interaction | lexical causative predicates may occur with the outer **dan** pattern | **dan + V-and** is reported as layered causation |
| Causal force | depends on lexical predicate/construction; not one universal scale | reported continuum from permissive/weak to directive/strong in the analytic causative |
| Dialect coverage | lexical and light-verb uses are broader; individual constructions still vary | analytic causative is specifically documented for Kurmanji and not established for Sorani in the cited survey |
| Key error to avoid | treating every N+kirin as a causative | treating every N+dan as an analytic causative |

The comparison concerns the constructional evidence in the cited sources. It is not a claim that **kirin** and **dan** exhaust all ways of expressing causation in Kurmanji.

## 6. Causee, causer and argument realization

A causative clause conceptually distinguishes:
- **causer**: the participant responsible for bringing about the event;
- **causee**: the participant caused, permitted, instructed or manipulated to act or undergo the event;
- **embedded participant(s)**: participants licensed by the embedded infinitive or lexical predicate.

However, these semantic roles do not map mechanically to one case form. Their realization depends on the exact construction, tense/aspect, lexical verb, and dialect. A source example must be analyzed as a whole.

The Akin & Bouveret example involving **dan + kirin + infinitive** is described as an external, agentive causative, with an agent/causer, an embedded action and an affected object/result. Their discussion of a **dan + V-and** example identifies the mothers as causer and the person stood up as causee. These are useful semantic diagnostics, but they do not establish a single case-marking template for every Kurmanji causative.

For any target-standard example, separately record:
1. causer's case and agreement behavior;
2. causee's case and syntactic position;
3. whether the causee is also the embedded subject;
4. embedded verb's valency and object;
5. finite agreement on the outer verb;
6. tense/aspect and polarity;
7. whether the causative is direct, indirect, permissive or manipulative.

Do not infer causee case from an English ‘make someone do something’ gloss.

## 7. Ordinary transitive clause versus analytic causative

A transitive clause can encode a causal event without a causative auxiliary. A lexical transitive verb may itself mean ‘break’, ‘raise’, ‘push’, ‘open’ or ‘move’. Such a clause should not be relabelled as **dan** causative or **kirin** causative simply because the event has a causer and an affected participant.

A useful classification test:
- Is there an overt causative auxiliary **dan** followed by an infinitive?
- Is there an overt **dan + kirin + infinitive** sequence?
- Is there an independently attested morphological causative form in **-and**?
- Is the predicate instead a lexical transitive verb?
- Is **kirin** merely part of a lexical N+kirin predicate?
- Does the construction have a conventionalized meaning not predictable from the parts?

Only the first three questions provide direct evidence for the analytic/morphological causative patterns under review.

## 8. Editorial and teaching decisions

- Teach **kirin** lexical ‘do/make’ separately from **kirin** as a light verb in lexical predicates.
- Teach N+kirin vocabulary as individual lexical entries; do not imply that all N+kirin combinations are causative.
- Teach **dan + infinitive** as an analytic Kurmanji causative construction, with contextual readings from permissive to directive.
- Teach **dan + kirin + infinitive** as a distinct complex causative, not as an ordinary N+kirin predicate.
- Teach **dan + V-and** as a source-attested layered causative pattern, with explicit restrictions and no invented productivity.
- Keep lexical transitivity, syntactic transitivity and semantic causation separate.
- Do not assign a universal case form to the causee; require a dialect-labelled attested example.
- Do not transfer the Kurmanji **dan** causative to Sorani.
- Keep Muş, Cizre/Botan-oriented standard, Behdinî and other local varieties labelled independently.
- Do not revise existing lessons or source content without explicit approval.

## 9. Evidence limits and unresolved questions

1. Akin & Bouveret's 2021 chapter is the central source for the three **dan** causative constructions and the force-of-causation analysis. Its examples come from their corpus and should not be treated as a complete dialect atlas.
2. The article's claim that the causative **dan** pattern was not elicited in their dialect survey and was absent in the Sorani data they discuss is a finding about their dataset, not proof that no speaker could ever produce a related form.
3. The exact causee case/agreement pattern requires construction-by-construction inspection of complete examples and additional grammatical descriptions. This audit deliberately does not invent a unified paradigm.
4. The morphological history of **-and** is unresolved in the cited discussion; no etymological derivation from **dan** is asserted.
5. The difference between direct, indirect, permissive and manipulative causation is contextual and should not be reduced to a one-word translation.
6. No elicitation with native speakers was conducted in this documentation pass.

## 11. Conclusion

The evidence supports a construction-specific account. **kirin** has lexical, light-verb and causative-light-verb uses; **dan** has lexical transfer, N+dan LVC, analytic causative and lexicalized compound uses. The clearest analytic contrast is between **dan + V-Inf**, **dan + V-and**, and **dan + kirin + V-Inf**. The case and agreement behavior of causer and causee must be read from each attested clause rather than projected from English translations.

**Decision:** maintain separate lexical and grammatical records for **kirin** and **dan**; classify causatives by their overt structure and attested semantics; keep dialect boundaries explicit; and do not insert generalized paradigms into the Academy's lessons without source and native-editor verification.
