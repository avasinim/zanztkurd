## 2. Main result

**All eight words currently used in Lesson 04 are documented in Haig and Öpengin (2018), §3.1.1, printed pp. 166–167.** The earlier QA note that `dil` was not found in the Haig example tables was too broad: `dil` is not in the short example tables for the five full vowels or the two weak-vowel tables on p. 166, but it is explicitly listed on p. 167 under the subsection on the lexical central vowel represented by standard orthographic `<i>`.

This resolves the narrow provenance question for `dil`; it does **not** by itself establish that the word is the best beginner contrast example, that its phonetic realization is uniform across varieties, or that the Kurmanji exercise wording is idiomatic.

## 4. Erratum to the earlier QA report

The earlier Lesson 04 QA item L04-03 said that `dil` was not found in the cited Haig tables and was found in the University of Arizona reference. That statement should be narrowed:

1. It is accurate that `dil` is not in the p. 166 tables listing the five full vowels and the two weak-vowel groups.
2. It is incomplete because Haig & Öpengin (2018), p. 167, separately lists `dil` under the lexical central vowel.
3. The source attribution for `dil` can therefore be Haig & Öpengin (2018), p. 167, rather than relying only on the Arizona overview.
4. The earlier uncertainty about the word's *pedagogical suitability* remains open. A qualified Kurmanji editor should decide whether `dil` is a good beginner example and whether the contrast task makes its intended orthographic point clearly.

This addendum does not alter or overwrite the earlier QA document; it records a dated correction so the audit trail remains visible.

## 5. Grapheme–phoneme distinction: a necessary limit

The lesson's safest beginner objective is recognition of **written vowel graphemes**. The evidence does not justify teaching a simplistic one-to-one mapping from each vowel letter to one invariant phone:

- Haig & Öpengin describe the written standard as practical and relatively uniform, but note that its orthography is parsimonious and does not represent every phonetic distinction.
- The same standard letter `<i>` is discussed in connection with distinct central-vowel behaviours, including a lexical central vowel and an epenthetic central vowel.
- Regional descriptions and transcription conventions differ in how central vowels are represented.

Therefore:

- Keep the learner objective orthographic: identify and preserve the written vowel letters in words.
- Define the *speech sound* and the *written letter* as different kinds of thing; do not define a vowel sound as a letter.
- Do not put IPA or a universal pronunciation claim into B0.
- Keep detailed phonetic categories in teacher/research notes until the reference variety and transcription policy are selected.

## 6. Assessment review: three contrast pairs

The existing exercise gives only one word for each pair:

- `e / ê` — `sêv`
- `i / î` — `spî`
- `u / û` — `bûk`

Each selected word contains only one member of its pair. The task as written does not require the learner to discriminate both members.

### Evidence-grounded candidate pair sets

The following are **candidate examples, not approved wording**. All are documented in Haig & Öpengin (2018), pp. 166–167:

| Contrast | Candidate pair | Source trace |

**Assessment-design recommendation:** present both words in each pair and ask learners to identify the written vowel grapheme in each word. The prompt and answer key must be written or approved by a proficient Kurmanji editor. The `dil / spî` pair needs particular care because the source treats `dil` under the lexical central vowel discussion; this is useful for grapheme recognition but must not be explained as a universal phonetic opposition without specialist review.

## 7. Dialect and standard-variety limits

Haig and Öpengin explicitly describe their standard Kurmanji orthography as based on the Botan spoken variety while also incorporating forms from elsewhere; they characterize it as a practical standard, not a pure transcript of one local dialect. Their account also notes regional vowel developments, including fronting of the full `/uː/` in some varieties.

Öpengin and Haig's 2014 dialect-classification study is based on lexical, phonological and morphosyntactic data from five localities in southeastern Turkey. It is an important warning against assuming that those locations exhaust all Kurmanji varieties.

Accordingly, this lesson may teach the standard written forms provisionally, but it must not claim that a single spoken realization is universal. A project owner/editorial decision is still required for the reference spoken variety.
