## Source coverage

### Ê — front mid/high vowel (printed pages 270–279)

The payload includes:
- Ê as a long, front, unrounded, close-mid vowel, IPA [e], with optional length marking [e:];
- its written forms in Kurdish-Arabic and Cyrillic scripts and comparison with European, Arabic, Persian and Turkish orthographies;
- occurrence at initial, medial and final word positions;
- the source's reported 5.2% frequency and its grammatical explanations, including feminine noun inflection, ezafe, plural ezafe, prepositions, future marking, post-verbal clitics and pronouns;
- historical origins of Ê, including retention of Middle Iranian Ê and developments from A in comparisons such as kêr/kard;
- Ê from Arabic loans and monophthongization of EY/YE, with examples such as fêris, kitêb, fêkî/fêqî, kêf, dêr and êm;
- Eastern/Central Kurmanji dialect correspondences, including zêvî/zevî, pênîr/penîr, mejî/mêjî and çerîn/çêrîn;
- Ê in inflection and ezafe, including noun forms, verb stems and the differences between Kurmanji and Sorani;
- vowel deletion and glide insertion in written and spoken inflected forms, including rê, perde, mase, mêze, name, Qamişlo, Holenda, Reşo, bira and xanî;
- alternations involving H, I and Î, including bihn/bîn, tihn/tîn and mihvan/mêvan;
- the source's account of Ê in European loans, its preference for retaining E in standard spelling, and examples such as telefon, kompûter, Internet, metod and demokrat;
- the source's cautions about writing Ê, including E/É substitutions and hypercorrection.

### I — short front-central vowel (printed pages 280–288)

The payload includes:
- I as a short, high, unrounded vowel, IPA [ɪ], distinguished from long Î and from Turkish [ɯ];
- the vowel's central-front articulation and its comparison with Arabic kasra;
- its spelling and the lack of a dedicated medial vowel mark in Kurdish-Arabic script;
- distribution and examples in medial and final positions, and the relative rarity of initial I in native vocabulary;
- dialectal initial forms such as iro, istêrk and istiran compared with îro, stêr/stêrk and stran;
- the source's discussion of initial I in Arabic and European loans and its recommendation not to insert Î without reason;
- the role of I in syllabification, including words such as bira, jiyan, ziman, siyas(et) and xiyanet where written vowels do not necessarily correspond to separate syllables;
- supportive I in pronunciation, including germ e, kevn e and nizm e, contrasted with its absence in standard spelling;
- I in inherited and borrowed vocabulary, including bihar, kirin, kilam, silav, bilbil, cindî and dinya;
- the author's argument against spelling based solely on speculative etymology, using bira, germ and kevn;
- consonant clusters at the beginnings of native and loan words, and the source's preference for preserving clusters rather than inserting an unnecessary vowel;
- the source's discussion of Î/I before Y and its preference for I in forms such as çiya, diyar, jiyan, kiriye, gotiye and xwariye, while distinguishing stems that genuinely contain Î.

### Î — long high front vowel (printed pages 289–297)

The payload includes:
- Î as a long, high, front, unrounded vowel, IPA [i], with optional length notation [i:];
- distinctions between Kurdish Î, Turkish İ and Turkish I/ı, and the relevant orthographic forms in Kurdish-Arabic and Cyrillic;
- occurrence in initial, medial and final positions, with examples îro, mîr, birîn, kî and masî;
- the source's reported frequency figure of 4.2% and grammatical sources including inflectional suffixes, pronouns, derivational suffixes, participles and temporal prefixes;
- Î in Eastern Kurmanji oblique forms and its distribution across dialects;
- historical sources of Î, including Middle Iranian Ê and monophthongized diphthongs, with examples pîr, kî, sîng, xanî, mîz, mî/mih and bîn;
- dialectal Î corresponding to Sorani Û, and variants involving IH, H deletion and Ü;
- the Dihok dialect changes Û → Î and O → Î, with the source's exceptions and footnotes;
- Î in verb inflection and ezafe, including çandin, standin/stendin, hejandin and xwandin/xwendin;
- Î → Û in selected Arabic loans such as mudîr → midûr and the proposed sequence mewlid → mewlîd → mewlûd;
- Arabic long Î versus short kasra, and the source's treatment of borrowed vocabulary and personal names;
- European-loan spelling, including the preference for stasyon, statistîk and stadyûm over forms with an added initial Î, and informasyon/imparator/televizyon over unnecessary E or Î substitutions;
- the source's recommendation to use Î chiefly in a stressed final syllable where the vowel is long.

## Source-fidelity observations

1. The payload follows the source's sequence and covers all three vowel sections: Ê, I and Î. The lesson boundary at printed page 298 is confirmed by the next section heading O.
2. The payload keeps the phonetic contrast between Ê [e], I [ɪ] and Î [i], and distinguishes vowel length from vowel quality.
3. The lesson preserves the author's separate treatments of historical change, dialectal variation, inflection and orthographic recommendation. These should not be conflated into universal rules.
4. The payload carries the source's comparisons and examples across Kurdish dialects and neighboring languages. Historical etymologies are represented as the author's explanations, not independently proven facts.
5. **Source transcription / internal error to flag:** on printed page 290 the book states “rêjeya herfa Ê ... 4,2%” in the paragraph about Î. The surrounding section is explicitly about Î, and the preceding E section has a separate 11.6% figure. The payload assigns 4.2% to Î, which appears to be a contextual correction of a likely source typo. The audit records this instead of treating the source wording as unambiguous.
6. **Source boundary nuance:** the lesson manifest starts at page 270 and ends at 297. Page 289 begins the Î subsection, not a new lesson; the next lesson begins at page 298. This is a valid grouping of the three vowel topics in the payload.
7. The source's own orthographic and etymological opinions (including recommendations about Ê/E, I/Î and initial clusters) remain attributed to the author. They are not presented as uncontested consensus.
8. The source's frequency figures are reported as given; the underlying corpus and counting method were not independently recreated.
9. This was a structured comparison of topics, examples and the lesson payload, not a line-by-line collation of every lexical form or independent verification of every reconstruction. Final rendered-page inspection for IPA, diacritics, Arabic script, tables and typography remains outstanding.
