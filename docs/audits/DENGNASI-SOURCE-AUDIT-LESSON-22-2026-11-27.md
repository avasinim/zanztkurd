## Source coverage reviewed

### 1. The two main writing systems and their forms (printed pages 348–350)

The payload covers:
- The two main contemporary Kurdish writing systems: Kurdish-Latin (KL) and Kurdish-Arabic (KE).
- The historical use of Cyrillic and the more limited use of Armenian and other scripts.
- The author's neutral purpose: comparison, not declaring one alphabet superior or imposing one script on all Kurds.
- The standard KL inventory of 31 letters and the historical variants such as ı/i, q/k, ch/sh, č/š, j/c, zh/j, é, ú/ü, ḧ and ẍ.
- The standard KE inventory and historical ways of representing short vowels and other sounds.
- The fact that KL and KE are not restricted to Kurmanji and Sorani respectively; both can be used for more than one Kurdish variety.
- Direction of writing, number and joining of letters, upper/lower case versus positional forms, vowel representation, shared and distinct consonant symbols, and initial eyn.

### 2. Similarities and differences between KL and KE (printed pages 349–351)

The payload includes the seven main differences and seven similarities:
- Direction, inventory size, letter joining and letter forms.
- Vowel representation, especially the non-direct representation of /i/ in KE and shared symbols for /î/–Y and /u/–W.
- The treatment of H, X, L and R distinctions and the initial eyn.
- The historical Phoenician connection as presented by the author.
- The common purpose of representing Kurdish sounds, the incomplete one-letter/one-sound fit of both scripts, and their open-ended capacity to add symbols.

### 3. Hevdeng, hevnivîs, hevwate and hevreh (printed pages 352–357)

The payload covers:
- Homophones with different meanings, including bar and ber, and the role of context.
- Homographs, including forms with different pronunciation because of aspiration/fortis-lenis distinctions and stress.
- Heterophonic homographs such as pir, par, birîn, ker and term.
- The difference between same-sound/different-spelling forms and synonyms.
- Proper-name capitalization examples such as jiyan/Jiyan and azad/Azad.
- Synonyms, near-synonyms, register, dialect and context differences.
- Related-root words and etymological doublets, including kirin/kirdin/kerden/kerdene and examples such as bajar/bazar, pîvan/peyman, hev/hem, peyv/peyam and nivîsîn/nivişte.

### 4. Kurdish–Persian phonological relations (printed pages 357–362)

The payload includes the author's comparative discussion of:
- A, B and V/W correspondences;
- D, Z and Y correspondences;
- E/I and short Ë versus Kurdish Î/Ê distinctions;
- G, H, M, O, Ş, Û, V/W, X, Z and J correspondences;
- the historical example zava/damad, including Sorani, Zazaki, Hewrami, Balochi, Avestan and Old Persian forms.

These are presented as the author's historical/phonological comparisons, not as independently verified reconstructions.

### 5. Whether Kurdish needs additional letters (printed pages 363–372)

The payload covers:
- The proposal to distinguish aspirated/unaspirated or fortis/lenis Ç/K/P/T and the author's reasons for retaining shared letters across varieties.
- Fortis and lenis R and the author's treatment of most cases as allophonic.
- Heavy/light L, its distribution across varieties, the possibility of ł or doubled L, and the author's preference for one shared L in unified writing.
- The two H realizations, the special distribution of the H in heft and derivatives, and dialectal variants in other words.
- Other variants such as Z in zayîn/zanîn and B in some dialects.
- The principle that no alphabet represents every pronunciation detail; dictionaries and teaching materials may provide phonetic precision while ordinary spelling remains conventional.
- The author's conclusion that adding letters would not clearly make Kurdish reading easier and could make spelling harder.
