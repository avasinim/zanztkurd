## Source-fidelity observations

1. The lesson follows the book's sequence: Eyn, K/G/Q, then consonant clusters. Its printed-page range 233–250 is consistent with the book's section divider for vowels on page 251.
2. The payload preserves the author's position on Eyn as an existing Kurdish sound while distinguishing that descriptive claim from the author's recommendation about everyday orthographic marking.
3. The K/G/Q section retains the source's distinctions among place, voicing and aspiration. In particular, aspirated and unaspirated K are not conflated with the K/G/Q contrast.
4. The discussion of Q retains the source's contact-language explanations as the author's historical interpretation, not as exceptionless sound laws.
5. The consonant-cluster section preserves the source's classification by lexical origin and the positional notation. It also records source-list irregularities: -XT appears in more than one origin grouping, and -XŞ is repeated in the Arabic-origin list.
6. **Potential editorial/source issue:** in the source's footnote 159, the Kurmanji K examples and the labels “nerm” are internally awkward: both aspirated and unaspirated forms are described with the same Kurdish term. The payload avoids reproducing that apparent wording error and instead preserves the IPA and aspiration distinction.
7. **Potential text transcription issue:** the book's European-loan examples on page 241 include forms whose spelling or transcription may require direct visual inspection of the printed page before treating every form as definitive. The payload should preserve the source examples as written, and this audit does not silently normalize them.
8. Historical reconstructions, contact explanations and lexical derivations are reported as the author's arguments. This audit does not independently establish each etymology.
