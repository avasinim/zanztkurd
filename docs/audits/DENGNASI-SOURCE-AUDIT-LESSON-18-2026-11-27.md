## Source coverage

### A — back open vowel (printed pages 252–255)

The payload includes:
- A as a low, back, unrounded vowel, represented in IPA by [ɑ], and the articulatory meaning of low/open, back and unrounded;
- the Kurdish-Arabic writing forms in initial, medial and final position, and the historical Cyrillic form;
- comparison with Turkish A, including the source's note on relative length;
- occurrence at the beginning, middle and end of words, with the source examples;
- the high frequency of A in medial position and its more restricted occurrence at the ends of simple, uninflected words;
- words whose written initial A is preceded by Eyn in pronunciation, such as aciz, alem, am and Amûdê;
- A in borrowed vocabulary, including amîn, aheng, axa, aman and demokrat.

### Changes involving A (printed pages 254–255)

The payload includes the three source groupings:
- historical change, including A → Ê comparisons such as hêk/hak, hêsan/asan and kêr/kard;
- dialectal change A → E, including dîsa/dîse, xwandin/xwendin, xwastin/xwestin, standin/stendin and the postposition variants da/de, ra/re and va/ve;
- dialectal rounding toward O before N in nan/non and dan/don;
- changes associated with inflection, including çandin → di-çîn-im, bijartin → di-bijêr-im and parastin → di-parêz-im;
- Central/Western Kurmanji inflectional A → Ê in agir/welat examples, contrasted with Eastern Kurmanji forms;
- dialectal addition of H before initial A in anîn/hanîn and avêtin/havêtin, with the source's explicit warning that this is not exceptionless;
- variation in Arabic-origin country and continent names, and the author's preference for retaining A in written standard forms.

### E — front open vowel (printed pages 256–269)

The payload includes:
- E as a low, front, unrounded vowel, IPA [æ], and its contrast with back A;
- Kurdish-Arabic and Cyrillic representations and the source's comparisons with Turkish, European languages, Arabic and Persian;
- occurrence at the beginning, middle and end of words, including ew, evîn, erzan, ber, xew, dev, te, bêje and parçe;
- the source's reported 11.6% frequency for E and the author's explanations involving verb endings, imperative suffixes and frequent pronouns;
- cases where E is written initially but Eyn is heard before it, such as erd, esman, ewr and Elî;
- historical comparisons involving Proto-Indo-European E and O, including deh/deka/decem/ten and heşt/octo comparisons;
- historical and borrowed-word changes E → I, including bihar/wesar/behar and kirin/kerdene/kerden, as well as alif, kilam and silav;
- E → A and E → I cases, including the source's comparisons serd/sar and kerd/kir, and its distinction between the two developments;
- monophthongization and diphthong-related examples, including EW/WE → O, EH → A, EY/YE → Ê, and Eyn + E / E + Eyn → A;
- the author's account of E → A under Turkish influence in some Arabic-origin words, distinguishing loans through Turkish from direct Arabic loans;
- analogy and dissimilation examples parçe/perçe, saet/seet/si'et and rahet/rehet/rihet;
- dialectal correspondences across Eastern, Central and Western Kurmanji and Sorani, including zêvî/zevî, pênîr/penîr, mejî/mêjî, çerîn/çêrîn, der/dir, keç/kiç and ez/az;
- changes associated with inflection in feminine nouns and verb paradigms, including mase-y-ê, rojname-y-ê, stendin → di-stîn-in and xwestin → di-xwaz-im;
- deletion of initial and final E in historical and inflected forms, including ber, wêran, yar, wê/wî/wan and imperative forms;
- restrictions on initial E and the addition of Eyn or H in forms such as ewr, hesp, heşt, esman and 'erd;
- E in European-origin vocabulary and the source's preference for retaining E in standard spelling, with examples telefon, kompûter, Internet, metod and demokrat;
- the source's recommendation to retain A rather than E in country and continent names such as Amerîka, Afrîka, Almanya, Albanya and Fransa.

## Source-fidelity observations

1. The payload follows the source order: A's articulation and distribution, changes involving A, E's articulation and distribution, then the historical, dialectal and inflectional changes involving E.
2. The main phonetic distinction is preserved: A is back [ɑ], E is front [æ]; both are open/low and unrounded.
3. The payload retains the author's distinctions among historical change, dialect variation and inflectional alternation. It does not present every dialect form as a universal Kurmanji rule.
4. The author's claims about Proto-Indo-European developments, loanword routes and historical sound changes are represented as the author's explanations. This audit does not independently validate every etymology or reconstruction.
5. The source's regional label “kurdiya başûrî” is explicitly restricted by the author to Feyli, Kalhuri, Kirmaşanî and related varieties, not all Kurdish varieties spoken in southern Kurdistan. The payload preserves this qualification.
6. The source's frequency values (A and E) are reported as the author's corpus-based findings. The underlying corpus and counting method were not independently re-created in this audit.
7. The payload includes the source's notes and examples on the A/E distinction and on the spellings of loanwords. No clear major section-level omission was identified in the inspected range.
8. **Editorial caution:** some of the source's historical comparisons and transcribed forms are complex and in places the source itself uses tentative wording. They should remain attributed to the author rather than rewritten as settled general linguistic facts.
9. **Textual limitation:** this was a structured comparison of source topics, examples and the lesson payload, not a word-for-word collation of every lexical item or every historical claim.
