The payload includes:
- the contrast between [h] and [ħ], with the source's word groups and the distinction between ordinary H and the emphatic/pharyngeal sound;
- the source's use of ḧ as a phonetic/linguistic notation to disambiguate the second sound, while retaining H in ordinary standard Latin spelling;
- Arabic-origin examples for [ħ], and the source's observation that this sound is retained in most Arabic loans;
- native Kurdish examples containing [ħ], including *ḧeft*, its derivatives, *teḧl/taḧl*, and *zeḧf/zaḧf*;

### H [h]: distribution and historical sources (printed pages 119–125)
The payload includes:
- H as a voiceless glottal fricative [h], its articulation and IPA notation;
- word-initial, medial, and final examples, and the author's reported 2.3% frequency;
- the contrast with Sorani, where H is generally absent word-finally;
- the distinction between inherited Proto-Indo-European H and H that arose later from other sounds;
- S → H before vowels in Iranian developments, with *heft/hew(t)* and comparisons to Indo-European forms;
- intervocalic D loss or change to H/Y, with *badam/bahîv* and *zaden/zan/zayîn* comparisons;
- word-initial H insertion, including *esp/hesp*, *jimare/hejmar*, *şotor/hêştir*, *asin/hesin*, and the *stêr → istêrk → histêrk* type of development;
- H corresponding to Persian X, and H-related correspondences with W and Ş in examples such as *hişk/xoşk*, *ceh/cow*, *têhnî/têşnê*, *çehv/çeşm*, and *rih/rîş*;
- H/W differences between Kurmanji and Sorani, the loss of H in vowel-plus-H sequences, and the change of *ih* to î;
- the source's example of Kurdish H becoming V in *şirove*.

- final J loss in *roj*, its compounds, *îro/evro*, *nîvro*, and the present stems *-bêj-* and *-avêj-*.

## Source-fidelity observations

1. The payload preserves the important distinction between [h] and [ħ] and does not present ḧ as a new standard spelling requirement. This is essential because the source recommends a single H in ordinary Kurmanji writing.
2. The payload records that [ħ] is not restricted to Arabic loans: it also occurs in native Kurdish words and in regional forms. It also preserves the Central/Eastern Kurmanji contrast.
3. The historical section distinguishes inherited Proto-Indo-European H from later H developments, including S → H, intervocalic D changes, and initial H insertion. These are not presented as one universal sound law.
4. The J section distinguishes the Kurdish sound [ʒ] from the differing letter values of J in English and several European languages.
5. The payload preserves the source's dialect and register qualifications for J → Ş and final J loss, rather than generalizing every example to all Kurmanji speakers.
6. Frequency values 2.3% for H and 2% for J are attributed to the source's cited studies, not presented as new corpus measurements.
7. Historical reconstructions and etymologies are represented as the book author's analysis. This audit checks correspondence to the book, not independent validity of every reconstruction.
