## 1. Why this reconciliation is necessary

The immediately preceding audits described the conditional system primarily through Thackston's reference-grammar account. That is useful evidence for the forms and constructions described there, but it must not be presented as a dialect-neutral template for all Northern Kurdish (NK/Kurmanji), and still less as a template for Central Kurdish (CK/Sorani).

Yaron Matras's comparative chapter, based on the Manchester Database of Kurdish Dialects (MDKD), provides a direct warning: NK and CK only partly share the inventory of conditional categories, and the combinations of verb forms in condition and consequence clauses differ across varieties. This report reconciles that comparative evidence with Thackston and records the implications for the proposed Kurmanji-only pathway.

## 2. Comparative corpus and source scope

Primary comparative source:

Yaron Matras. “The typology of connectivity and complex construction in the dialects of Kurdish.” In Yaron Matras, Geoffrey Haig & Ergin Öpengin (eds.), *Structural and Typological Variation in the Dialects of Kurdish*. Cham: Palgrave Macmillan, 2021. Chapter PDF consulted, especially §5.6 “Conditional,” printed pp.52–56, and §5.7, pp.56–57.

The chapter reports data from the Manchester Database of Kurdish Dialects:
- approximately 50,000 words of transcribed speech;
- speakers from 71 locations;
- 44 NK/Kurmanji-Bahdini varieties and 25 CK varieties;
- elicitation phrases plus a smaller corpus of free speech.

The author cautions that conditional coverage in the questionnaire corpus is not comprehensive. It is nevertheless a comparative source with identifiable locations and examples, not merely a prescriptive paradigm.

## 3. First distinction: real versus unreal conditions

Matras distinguishes:
- **real/possible conditions**: the condition remains possible and its consequence may become true;
- **unreal/counterfactual conditions**: the condition can no longer be fulfilled and is counterfactual;
- an intermediate **potential/unlikely but still possible** category.

The intermediate category matters: it is not safely reducible to either ordinary real condition or fully counterfactual condition.

## 4. Real conditions: shared protasis, different consequence

For the elicitation “If you do not work much, you will remain poor,” Matras reports a consistent present-subjunctive protasis. The consequence differs:

| Variety group | Condition/protasis | Consequence/apodosis |
|---|---|---|
| Most NK varieties | Present subjunctive | Future tense |
| Five NK samples | Present subjunctive | Present indicative |
| CK varieties | Present subjunctive | Present indicative |

The chapter gives a Northern sample from Kozluk, Turkey (K068), and a Central Kurdish sample from Urmia, Iran (S040). These are not interchangeable surface templates: the Northern consequence contains a future marker, while the Central sample uses present progressive/indicative morphology.

**Editorial consequence:** a general rule “present subjunctive in the if-clause, future in the result” overgeneralizes to CK; a general rule “present subjunctive + present indicative” underrepresents most NK varieties.

## 5. Unreal/counterfactual conditions: distinct regional configurations

For the elicitation “If I had known that you would not come to the wedding, I would not have gone there either,” the chapter reports the following:

| Sample | Location | Condition/protasis | Consequence/apodosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| K026 | Uludere, Turkey | `mi zanî-ba` (I.OBL know-PST.COND) | Negative past imperfect |
| S052 | Kamyaran, Iran | `zanî-ba-m` | Negative past imperfect |
| K108 | Kelhê, Syria | `bi-zani-ya` | Negative past imperfect |
| S075 | Khalakan, Iraq | `bi-m-zany-aye` | Negative past imperfect |
| S038 | Naqadeh, Iran | Imperfect | Imperfect |
| G001 | Sahneh, Iran | Imperfect | Imperfect |

The table illustrates several points:

1. The condition clause can show a past conditional/past-subjunctive form with differing suffixes and prefixes across regions.
2. Some CK/SK samples in Iran use the imperfect in both clauses; Matras suggests possible convergence with Persian, which lacks a conditional tense/mood.
3. A single form such as `bi-zanî-ba` cannot be declared the universal form for every Kurdish dialect.
4. The result clause is not automatically another past perfect conditional: the examples above use an imperfective past construction.

## 6. Intermediate “unlikely but potentially possible” conditions

For “If you had worked hard, you would have earned a lot of money,” Matras finds a split:

- most samples use a conditional or past-conditional form in the condition clause;
- some CK and SK samples in Iran again use the imperfect on both sides;
- in the consequence clause, NK samples show the **future perfect**, while CK samples show the imperfect.

Examples include:
- K024 Tatvan, Turkey: conditional in the condition clause and a future-marked conditional form in the consequence;
- K051 Mosul, Iraq: past conditional in both condition and future-marked consequence;
- S075 Khalakan, Iraq: past conditional condition, imperfect consequence;
- S047 Oshnaviyeh, Iran: past conditional condition, present/imperfective existential consequence.

Matras explicitly describes this intermediate category as separately represented in NK but not in CK in the elicited data. Therefore, a learner-facing course should not flatten this into the same pattern as a fully counterfactual “if I had known…” sentence.

## 7. Northern Kurdish is internally variable: Bahdini matters

Matras identifies regional differences inside NK:
- Turkey and northern Syria: forms such as `bi-zanî-bûya`;
- some NK varieties in Iraq: `zanî-ba` or `di-zanî-ya`;
- Bahdini varieties: future-perfect forms such as `dê (bi)zanî-ye`;
- in the consequence clause, Turkey/Syria and Mosul samples tend to repeat an overt subject pronoun; Bahdini samples show verb agreement but no overt pronoun, placing them between broader groupings.

This matters directly to any proposal to use a loosely Cizre/Botan-oriented written norm while also representing a separate Bahdini pathway. A course must select its reference norm and label regional alternatives; it cannot simply blend all attested forms into one paradigm.

## 8. Reconciliation with the earlier Thackston audits

Thackston §§25–25.2 gives a detailed pedagogical account of the past conditional, past perfect conditional and future conditional/future perfect patterns in the reference norm described by that grammar.

Matras does not invalidate the existence of those forms. Rather, the comparative corpus limits how far we may generalize them:

| Earlier audit claim / framing | Comparative correction |
|---|---|
| Past counterfactual protasis uses a past perfect conditional | True for the Thackston account and some patterns; comparative NK/CK samples also show other past-conditional forms and imperfect-based patterns |
| Apodosis may use past perfect or future perfect conditional | This is not a dialect-neutral rule; Matras documents NK future-perfect consequences for an intermediate potential category, but CK imperfect consequences |
| Two past-perfect conditional variants are dialect-conditioned | Supported in spirit; Matras documents additional regional shapes and does not reduce the full NK/CK map to only two forms |
| `bûn` and `karîn` require special selection rules | Keep as Thackston-specific reference-grammar guidance until separately corroborated in the selected regional variety |
| One “past counterfactual” template can be used for the entire course | Not supported; the course must choose and consistently label a reference variety |

The previous audit should therefore be read as a **Thackston reference-grammar analysis**, not as a comprehensive description of all Kurmanji dialects. This reconciliation is the controlling caveat for any future curriculum proposal.

## 9. Recommended policy for the fully Kurmanji-medium pathway

Proposal only; no project-owner variety decision is implied.

1. Select one primary written reference variety before writing B3 conditional lessons.
2. If the chosen norm is Northern Kurmanji oriented to Cizre/Botan, identify that as the course's pedagogical target, not as the only authentic Kurmanji.
3. Keep Bahdini and other Iraqi varieties in clearly labelled comparison notes where relevant; do not merge their forms into the default answer key.
4. Keep CK/Sorani examples out of the Kurmanji-only learner sequence unless an explicitly comparative contrast is intended.
5. Separate three semantic categories in the syllabus: real/possible, unreal/counterfactual, and potential/unlikely-but-still-possible.
6. Teach the condition and consequence clauses as a paired system. Do not assign a single tense form to the whole sentence.
7. Keep Thackston's detailed paradigms labelled by source and norm; use Matras's corpus examples as comparative evidence, not as a new prescriptive standard.
8. Require a qualified Kurmanji editor from the selected reference variety to review all examples and the chosen orthography before any learner-facing use.

## 10. Evidence grades and limits

| Finding | Evidence | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| NK and CK only partly share conditional categories | Direct comparative statement, Matras §5.6 | Corpus coverage of complex clauses is not exhaustive |
| Real condition protasis uses present subjunctive | Direct comparative elicitation result | Consequence differs by variety |
| Most NK real-condition consequences are future; CK consequences present indicative | Direct corpus summary | Five NK samples show present indicative |
| Unreal-condition protasis forms vary by region | Direct examples from six locations | Elicitation set is limited |
| Some Iranian CK/SK samples use imperfect in both clauses | Direct examples and author's convergence hypothesis | Persian influence is suggested, not proven causally |
| NK intermediate-potential consequences show future perfect; CK shows imperfect | Direct comparative summary and examples | Sample-level generalization, not universal law |
| Exact acceptability for the project's target variety | Unresolved | Needs local speaker/editor validation |
