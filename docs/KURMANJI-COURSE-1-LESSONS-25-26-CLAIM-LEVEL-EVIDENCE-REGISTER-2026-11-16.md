## 1. Method and evidence labels

This register separates (a) the grammatical or orthographic proposition, (b) evidence for the proposition, and (c) whether the exact teaching example is independently attested. A source supporting a rule does not automatically authenticate every example sentence or Sorani gloss.

- **SUPPORTED** — directly corroborated by a relevant Kurmanji source.
- **PARTLY SUPPORTED** — central proposition is supported, but scope, wording, variety, or exact example needs qualification.
- **UNRESOLVED** — evidence checked is insufficient to settle the exact claim/example.
- **EDITORIAL** — linguistic evidence may be sound, but Sorani wording, terminology, or pedagogy needs a separate native-editor decision.

Primary anchors used here:
1. Haig & Öpengin (2015), “Gender in Kurdish: Structural and socio-cultural dimensions,” especially §§2.1.1–2.1.2, pp. 250–251. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1075/impact.36.10hai ; author-hosted offprint: https://www.uni-bamberg.de/fileadmin/aspra/06_Team/Publications_and_CV/haig_opengin_2015_gender_kurdish_offprint.pdf

M., *Kurmanji Kurdish: A Reference Grammar with Selected Readings*, “The Phonology of Kurmanji Kurdish,” pp. 1–3; and §§18.2–18.3 on past transitive/ergative constructions: https://sites.fas.harvard.edu/~iranian/Kurmanji/kurmanji_complete.pdf (accessible text mirror: https://www.academia.edu/7363808/Kurmanji_Kurdish_A_Reference_Grammar_with_Selected_Readings)

The 2015 article explicitly describes the widely accepted written standard variety for its examples, while also noting that Kurmanji has regional varieties. Baker & Atlamaz's core case-and-agreement examples are from Adıyaman Kurmanji, described as matching standard/literary Kurmanji in relevant respects; their paper separately documents a double-oblique pattern in Muş and around Diyarbakır. These sources must not be collapsed into a single undifferentiated “all dialects” rule.

## 2. Lesson 25 — direct and oblique case

### C25-01 — Kurmanji has a two-way direct/oblique case opposition
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig & Öpengin (2015, §2.1.1, pp. 250–251) describe an unmarked Direct case and a marked Oblique case in nouns and pronouns. Baker & Atlamaz (2014, §1.2) likewise adopt the traditional Iranian-linguistics terms “direct” and “oblique.”

**Editorial consequence:** retain Direct/Oblique as the principal course terminology. Do not casually rename them nominative/accusative.

### C25-02 — Direct and oblique are not simple synonyms for nominative and accusative
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig & Öpengin explicitly caution that Direct and Oblique cannot simply be equated with nominative and accusative, because the distribution changes across present and past transitive clauses. Baker & Atlamaz also show that the same oblique form serves the roles often called ergative and accusative in different clause types.

**Editorial consequence:** the lesson's note explaining that these are grammatical case labels, not everyday meanings, is useful. Preserve it.

### C25-03 — Masculine and feminine singular oblique forms
**Status: SUPPORTED, WITH VARIETY QUALIFICATION**

Haig & Öpengin (2015, §2.1.1) give feminine singular oblique `-ê` and masculine singular oblique `-î` in their standard paradigm, including `jin-ê`, `mêrîk-î`, and indefinite `jin-ek-ê` / `kur-ek-î`.

Baker & Atlamaz (2014, note 4) clarify a significant dialect distinction: Adıyaman Kurmanji uses `-ê` for both masculine and feminine singular nouns in their examples, while the standard variety and Muş have `-ê` on feminine nouns and zero marking for masculine singular nouns.

**Editorial consequence:** the lesson's `gund / gund-î` and `jin / jin-ê` paradigm is supported for the intended standard paradigm. Its caveat about zero-marked masculine forms is necessary. Do not generalize one suffix table to all Kurmanji dialects.

### C25-04 — Indefinite `-ek` can combine with oblique case marking
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig & Öpengin (2015, §2.1.1) explicitly give `jin-ek-ê` and `kur-ek-î`. This supports the lesson's structural point that the indefinite suffix can be followed by the oblique case marker.

**Editorial consequence:** retain the pattern, but avoid implying every noun has exactly the same surface form across varieties.

### C25-05 — Personal pronouns have direct and oblique forms
**Status: SUPPORTED, WITH PARADIGM CHECK**

Haig & Öpengin (2015, §2.1.1) explicitly confirm third-person singular oblique `wî` masculine and `wê` feminine, with no gender distinction in first/second person or plural. Baker & Atlamaz (2014, examples and pronoun table) corroborate the direct/oblique contrast, although their transcription and some dialectal forms differ from the lesson's pedagogical table.

**Editorial consequence:** the lesson's `ez/min`, `tu/te`, `ew/wî,wê`, `em/me`, `hûn/we`, `ew/wan` is consistent with the standard teaching paradigm. The third-person direct `ew` is syncretic across gender/number and needs context; retain that note. Before treating the whole table as universally invariant, confirm the exact selected reference variety.

### C25-06 — Present transitive clauses: subject Direct, object Oblique
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Baker & Atlamaz (2014, §1.1, examples (2), (6a), and table (8)) directly show the present-tense transitive subject in Direct case and object in Oblique case, with agreement on the subject. Haig & Öpengin (2015, §2.1.1) state the same contrast.

**Editorial consequence:** the rule is sound as a standard/neutral teaching pattern. Keep “usually” or a clearly scoped standard-variety label rather than “always.”

### C25-07 — Past transitive clauses: subject Oblique, object Direct
**Status: SUPPORTED FOR THE CANONICAL PATTERN; NOT UNIVERSAL**

Baker & Atlamaz (2014, §1.1, examples (4), (6b), and table (8)) and Haig & Öpengin (2015, §2.1.1) support the canonical past-transitive split. The verb agrees with the Direct object in the standard Adıyaman pattern discussed by Baker & Atlamaz.

However, Baker & Atlamaz §§3.3–3.4 document a double-oblique past-transitive pattern in Muş Kurmanji and in the dialect around Diyarbakır: both transitive arguments may be Oblique and the verb may default to third-person singular. This is a substantive dialect difference, not a minor spelling variant.

**Editorial consequence:** the lesson already calls the rule a basic pattern and acknowledges variation. Keep this qualification adjacent to the rule. If the course later selects a reference variety, label the canonical paradigm accordingly.

### C25-08 — Past transitive verb agreement is with the Direct object in the canonical pattern
**Status: SUPPORTED, WITH DIALECT AND CONSTRUCTION LIMITS**

Baker & Atlamaz (2014, abstract, §1.1 and table (8)) directly state that the past transitive verb agrees with the Direct object in their Adıyaman data. The Muş double-oblique example (their (60c), `Min te dit`) instead has default third-singular agreement, illustrating why the lesson's caveat matters.

**Editorial consequence:** keep “typically/in the canonical pattern”; do not make it an exceptionless rule.

### C25-09 — Exact example `Ez wî mirovî dibînim`
**Status: PARTLY SUPPORTED**

The University of Arizona Kurmanji overview gives this sentence as an attested teaching example with the meaning “I see that man,” supporting the lesson's use of a present transitive subject in Direct and object phrase in Oblique. Haig & Öpengin independently support the morphology of masculine singular oblique `wî` and `-î`.

**Editorial consequence:** the sentence's central case analysis is supported. Preserve its spelling only after checking it against the selected house reference variety and keep the Sorani translation in the separate editorial queue.

### C25-10 — Exact example `Min ew dît`
**Status: PARTLY SUPPORTED; VERBATIM ATTESTATION NOT ESTABLISHED**

The standard past-transitive analysis predicts an Oblique agent and a Direct patient. The example is structurally plausible and fits the lesson's stated canonical pattern. However, the exact sentence `Min ew dît` was not located verbatim in the sources checked. Baker & Atlamaz provide directly attested past-transitive examples such as `Mi ti di-yi` for Adıyaman and `Min te dit` for the Muş double-oblique pattern; the latter is not evidence for the lesson's exact standard sentence.

**Editorial consequence:** identify `Min ew dît` as an instructor-created pedagogical example unless a Kurmanji specialist confirms it or an exact published attestation is found. Do not present it as a source quotation. Check the inflection and dialect before reuse.

### C25-11 — Sorani definitions, glosses, and explanations
**Status: EDITORIAL**

This linguistic review does not certify Sorani spelling, punctuation, or translation quality. The lesson includes Sorani terminology and translations that require a dedicated native Sorani copy-edit.

**Editorial consequence:** no automatic replacement of Sorani forms. Wait for the approved Avasin house style and native-editor review.

## 3. Lesson 26 — recap, orthography, and self-assessment

### C26-01 — The common Hawar/Kurmanji Latin alphabet has 31 letters
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Thackston's phonology chapter presents the standard Kurmanji Latin letter inventory. The Institute of the Estonian Language's KNAB reference identifies the Kurmanji Roman alphabet as Hawar 1932 and lists the standard inventory. Secondary overview literature also describes the 31-letter Hawar alphabet.

**Editorial consequence:** keep the inventory at 31 letters. A doubled spelling such as `rr` must not silently become a 32nd alphabet letter.

### C26-02 — `rr` is not an additional Hawar alphabet letter
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Thackston (phonology, pp. 1–3) distinguishes flap and trill phonetic realizations and says the trill is only sporadically represented in orthography by `rr`; the example given is `pir`, sometimes written `pirr`. This is a spelling convention/digraph in limited usage, not a separate letter in the 31-letter inventory.

The University of Arizona overview likewise says the trill/flap distinction is often ignored in orthography and that when observed the trill is indicated by `rr`.

**Editorial consequence:** refine the lesson's sentence so it does not imply `rr` is an extra alphabet letter or a universally obligatory spelling distinction.

### C26-03 — The University of Arizona `rr` table row and `roj` example
**Status: SOURCE-INTERNAL INCONSISTENCY; CLAIM MUST BE NARROWED**

The Arizona overview's prose says that the trill may be written `rr`, but its table row labelled `rr` gives `roj` (“day”), spelled with a single `r`. That row is internally inconsistent and cannot be used as decisive evidence that `roj` should be written with `rr`.

Thackston offers an independent, more precise description: word-initial `r` is trilled, and the trill is only sporadically marked by `rr`, with `pir/pirr` as the example. Thus the two sources together support a qualified phonetic/orthographic note, not a blanket rule or an emendation of `roj`.

**Editorial consequence:** do not change `roj`; do not write `rroj`; do not invent a minimal pair. State that `rr` is an occasional representation of a trill in some descriptions/orthographic practices, while the standard alphabet inventory remains 31 letters.

### C26-04 — `c/ç`, `s/ş`, `i/î`, and `u/û` are meaningful contrasts
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Thackston's phonology chapter gives distinct phonetic values for these letters: `c` vs `ç`, `s` vs `ş`, and the short/long or quality distinctions represented by `i/î` and `u/û`. The Arizona overview independently lists corresponding contrasts and examples.

**Editorial consequence:** retain the teaching point that these are not interchangeable spellings. Keep pronunciation descriptions aligned with the selected reference variety and avoid claiming every dialect realizes every phonetic distinction identically.

### C26-05 — Vocabulary examples `çak`, `şaş`, `dil`, `îro`, and `roj`
**Status: PARTLY SUPPORTED**

The Arizona overview provides `çak` (“good”), `şaş` (“wrong”), `dil` (“heart”), and `îro` (“today”); Thackston also provides `dil` and `îro` in its phonology examples and `roj` appears in the Arizona table as “day,” albeit under the inconsistent `rr` row. The spelling `roj` itself should be retained as shown in the lesson, but the Arizona table row must not be treated as evidence for an `rr` spelling.

The contrast `şaş` (“wrong”) vs `şeş` (“six”) is a lexical distinction and should not be blurred in proofreading.

**Editorial consequence:** the listed glosses have useful support, but this is not a complete dictionary audit of every phrase or sentence in the lesson.

### C26-06 — `Ez naxwînim` gloss
**Status: PARTLY SUPPORTED / CONTEXT-SENSITIVE**

The form is presented as a negative present-tense example. Its Sorani gloss is understandable in the intended reading context, but the exact semantic equivalent depends on context and the verb's reading/studying sense.

**Editorial consequence:** avoid presenting one Sorani or English gloss as the only possible meaning in every context. Native Kurmanji and Sorani review is required.

### C26-07 — Neutral word order is often SOV, but order is not rigid
**Status: SUPPORTED AT THE GENERAL LEVEL**

Haig (2018) and the prior source review support SOV as a neutral/default tendency while allowing information-structure and argument-structure variation. Lesson 26 already warns that not every clause has an object and that focus can affect order.

**Editorial consequence:** keep the caveat; do not turn SOV into a categorical rule. A more advanced course may introduce the Goal/recipient position (SOVG) with carefully checked examples.

### C26-08 — Final self-assessment is informal, not certification
**Status: SUPPORTED AS PEDAGOGICAL DESIGN**

The lesson explicitly says the self-assessment is not a formal grade or proficiency certification. This is an appropriate limitation statement. The review does not evaluate the psychometric validity of the checklist as a formal assessment instrument.

**Editorial consequence:** preserve the informal disclaimer. Do not advertise the checklist as a proficiency test or certificate.

### C26-09 — Full recap table and all sample sentences
**Status: PARTLY SUPPORTED**

The recap covers previously taught content areas (alphabet, word order, questions, negation, pronouns, and case). The broad topics align with the course syllabus and source reviews. This register did not independently re-verify every sentence, every lexical item, or every Sorani gloss in the recap.

**Editorial consequence:** do not treat the recap's inclusion in this register as a full sentence-by-sentence validation. Retain the existing action register's separate native-editor and example-verification tasks.

2. Keep direct/oblique labels; do not reduce them to subject/object or nominative/accusative.
3. Preserve the canonical present/past transitive alignment, but state the variety scope and keep the Muş/Diyarbakır double-oblique caveat available in teacher notes or advanced material.

5. Keep the 31-letter inventory separate from the occasional `rr` digraph/convention.
6. Do not infer an `rr` spelling for `roj` from the inconsistent Arizona table row; no minimal pair should be added without independent verification.
7. Keep the phonetic tap/trill distinction separate from the question of whether a given word's spelling marks it.

- Keep seed/migration parity as a separate technical gate; this register does not resolve or test it.
