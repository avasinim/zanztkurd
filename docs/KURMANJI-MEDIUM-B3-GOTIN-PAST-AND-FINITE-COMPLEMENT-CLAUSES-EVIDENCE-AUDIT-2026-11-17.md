## 1. Scope and questions

This addendum examines four issues that must not be collapsed into one rule:

1. The ordinary canonical past-transitive pattern of `gotin` ‘say’.
2. A special construction with a finite complement after past ‘say’ or ‘see’, where Thackston reports loss of ergativity.
3. The mood of finite subordinate clauses with verbs of speech or thought.
4. The temporal interpretation of clauses following past verbs of perception.

The evidence shows that these are related but distinct phenomena. In particular, the special `Min gotim ku…` construction is not a licence to teach every past-transitive `gotin` clause with subject agreement, and the tense of a perception complement is not the same issue as case/agreement in the matrix clause.

## 2. Ordinary past-transitive `gotin`: direct evidence

Thackston, §18.2, pp. 47–49, describes the canonical simple past of transitive verbs as ergative: the agent is Oblique, the patient is Direct/nominative, and the verb agrees with the patient. The rule is general; the examples include `gotin`.

### 2.1 Singular patient

Thackston gives:

- `Mêrîk bi kenekî ne ji dil got…` — ‘The fellow said with a smile, not from the heart…’ (§18.2, p. 48).
- `Gundiyan tiştêk negot.` — ‘The villagers didn’t say anything.’ (§18.2, p. 48).

The second sentence is particularly useful because it displays a plural Oblique agent (`gundiyan`), an indefinite Direct patient (`tiştêk`), and the 3sg negative verb `negot`. Haig & Öpengin independently reproduce the example in their 2018 grammar sketch as `Gundi-yan tişt-ek ne-got`, glossing the verb NEG-say.PST.3SG (p. 190).

**Evidence status:** directly attested ordinary negative past-transitive `gotin` with a plural agent and a 3sg patient. This is not a six-person conjugation table.

### 2.2 A plural form that is not ordinary patient agreement

Haig & Öpengin (2018), p. 190, give a written example from Cegerxwîn:

`Herdu çûne cem rovî doza xwe jê re gotin.`

Their interlinear analysis identifies `got-in` as SAY.PST-PL. The two-person subject (`herdu`) is not repeated overtly before `gotin`; it is understood from the immediately preceding intransitive clause. The patient `doz` ‘case’ is singular. Haig & Öpengin explain that the plural verb here reflects the plural subject carried over from the preceding intransitive clause, not plural agreement with the singular patient. They report this pattern for spoken and written varieties when a transitive clause is preceded by an intransitive clause with the same subject, while noting that the broader extent of subject agreement varies by dialect, verb semantics and syntactic factors (pp. 190–191).

**Critical teaching boundary:** do not present `gotin` as always agreeing with the patient in every attested past clause, nor present this special discourse-linked plural as a general alternative paradigm. The canonical patient-agreement rule remains the baseline; the Cegerxwîn example is a separately conditioned pattern.

## 3. Special finite-complement construction: `Min gotim ku…`

Thackston, §18.5, p. 51, explicitly states that ergativity may be lost for stylistic reasons in past transitive verbs, normally in expressions such as ‘I said that…’ and ‘I saw that…’ when the verb is followed by a subordinate clause functioning as its complement:

- `Min gotim ku…` — ‘I said that…’ (contrasted by Thackston with `min got`).
- `Min dîtim ku…` — ‘I saw that…’ (contrasted with `min dît`).

Thackston then says that otherwise tenses and moods built on the past stem of transitive verbs are normally ergative, while some eastern dialects sporadically show loss of ergativity and the criteria for that broader loss have not been determined.

This is a narrowly described construction, not a blanket replacement of the canonical past-transitive pattern. The matrix clause contains a finite complement introduced by `ku`; the complement itself has its own predicate and temporal/mood interpretation.

### 3.1 Evidence strength and limits

- The two first-person examples are **directly printed** by Thackston in §18.5.
- The contrast with ordinary `min got` / `min dît` is explicit in that source.
- The source characterizes the pattern as stylistic and normally tied to a following subordinate complement.
- The source does not provide a full paradigm across all persons, all complement types, or all Kurmanji regional varieties.
- Do not derive a six-person `gotin` or `dîtin` paradigm from these two examples.
- Do not assume that every clause containing `ku` triggers the same construction; the source's description concerns a complement clause after the relevant past speech/perception verb.

## 4. Mood inside speech/thought complements

Haig & Öpengin (2018), p. 187, state that subordinate clauses with verbs of speech or thought may occur in the subjunctive or may occur without it, depending on the degree of certainty of the proposition expressed.

Their examples include:

- `Dît ko gotina wî rast e.` — ‘He saw that what he said was right.’ (example 23; the embedded predicate is a copular indicative form).
- `De bêje te çawan kir.` — ‘Go on, say how you did it.’ (example 24; an embedded past form).
- `Nizanîbû ko çawan bê pere vegere mal.` — ‘He didn’t know how he would return home without any money.’ (example 25; embedded present subjunctive).

The chapter's point is not that all speech complements freely alternate without constraints. Rather, mood selection is sensitive to the speaker's presentation of the proposition, especially its certainty. These examples also show that the embedded clause may be indicative, past, or subjunctive according to its function and interpretation.

**Boundary:** this mood choice is separate from the matrix-clause ergativity exception in Thackston §18.5. Haig & Öpengin's examples support mood variation in speech/thought complements; they do not, by themselves, establish a full paradigm for `Min gotim ku…`.

## 5. Sequence of tenses after past perception verbs

Thackston §33, p. 78, separately describes the temporal interpretation of complements after past verbs of perception (seeing, hearing, thinking, realizing, feeling, guessing, etc.). Unlike English, Kurmanji may retain the tense that would have been used at the time of the perception, rather than automatically shifting it back.

A directly printed example is:

`Min dîtim ku mamoste ne tê ye.`

Thackston translates this as ‘I saw that the teacher was not there’ and explains that the embedded clause reflects the contemporaneous thought ‘The teacher is not here.’ The embedded present `ye` is therefore not an accidental failure to backshift; it reflects the perspective at the time of perception.

Other examples in §33 include `Wî hîs kir ku tenê ye` (‘He felt he was alone’) and `Ez têgihîştim ku zimanekî min yê taybetî heye` (‘I came to the realization that I had a special language’).

**Do not conflate:**
- §18.5: a special matrix past form in a complement construction (`Min gotim/dîtim ku…`) and the reported loss of ergativity.
- §33: the temporal viewpoint and tense selected inside a perception complement.
- Haig & Öpengin p. 187: mood choice in complements of speech/thought verbs.

These can co-occur in a sentence, but they answer different grammatical questions.

## 7. Editorial recommendations (proposal only)

1. Keep the canonical past-transitive pattern as the initial teaching baseline: Oblique agent, Direct patient, patient agreement.
2. Teach `Gundiyan tiştêk negot` as a directly attested negative example, with the roles explicitly labelled.
3. Keep the Cegerxwîn `gotin` plural example in an advanced note on discourse-linked subject agreement; do not turn it into the default rule or an unrestricted dialect-neutral paradigm.
4. Introduce `Min gotim ku…` only as a source-attributed, construction-specific exception, pending review by a qualified native Kurmanji editor.
5. In a separate section, explain that mood in speech/thought complements can vary with certainty; do not claim that `ku` mechanically selects subjunctive.
6. Teach temporal viewpoint after past perception verbs separately from matrix case/agreement. Preserve the contrast between English translation and Kurmanji embedded tense.
7. Keep dialect and register labels attached to each example. Haig & Öpengin's broader claims concern a grammar sketch of Kurmanji in Turkey and document dialect variation; Thackston's prescriptions/examples represent a particular pedagogical reference grammar.
8. Do not update existing lesson bodies, seed records, or the approved course standard on the basis of this audit alone.
