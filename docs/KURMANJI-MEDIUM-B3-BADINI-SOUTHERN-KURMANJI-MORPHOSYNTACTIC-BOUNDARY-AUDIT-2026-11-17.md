# Badini / Behdinî and Southern Kurmanji: Morphosyntactic Boundary Audit
Date: 2026-11-17
Repository branch: `fix/kurmanji-ci-current-migration`
Scope: research documentation only; no lesson, seed, application, migration, CI, production database, PR, or deployment changes.

## Executive finding

Badini/Behdinî is a southeastern Kurmanji variety with substantial shared structure with other Kurmanji varieties, but the available comparative sources document a distinct TAM/modal subsystem in at least the future and counterfactual domains. It must not be treated as a spelling variant of the Cizîr/Botan-oriented Standard Kurmanjî reference norm.

For Pathway B, the defensible provisional architecture remains:
- Teach the selected written reference norm consistently in the main instructional sequence.
- Put attested Behdinî/Southeastern Kurmanji alternatives in clearly labelled dialect notes, not as interchangeable answers in a single paradigm.
- Do not infer an entire Badini paradigm from one particle or one example.
- Do not finalise a dialect policy without owner/editor approval and native-speaker review.

## 1. Sources and evidential reach

### A. Christoph Unger (2014), “Badini Kurdish modal particles dê and da: procedural semantics and language variation”
*Kurdish Studies* 2(2), 177–200. DOI: 10.67145/ks.v2i2.64.

This is the most directly relevant source for the modal particles discussed here. Unger explicitly compares Badini, predominantly spoken in the Dohuk Governorate of Iraq, with the Standard Kurmanji norm described in Bedir Khan and Lescot and represented in Latin-script publications and a contemporary newspaper corpus. The paper's Badini examples are drawn from recorded stories and published material; it is a focused study of modal particles, not a complete grammar of all Behdinî varieties.

### B. Geoffrey Haig (2018), “Northern Kurdish (Kurmanjî)”
In *The Languages and Linguistics of Western Asia*, chapter 2.3.

Haig distinguishes the Standard Kurmanjî sketch from the Behdinī material treated separately, describes Behdinī as part of the southeastern end of a dialect continuum, and documents regional variation in morphology, agreement, case, and TAM. This supports treating Behdinī as a systematic regional variety rather than an undifferentiated exception list.

### C. Öpengin & Haig (2014), “Regional variation in Kurmanji: A preliminary classification of dialects”
*Kurdish Studies* 2(2), 143–176. DOI: 10.67145/ks.v2i2.62.

This provides a comparative regional framework based on lexical, phonological and morphosyntactic data from five localities in southeastern Turkey. It is useful for cautioning against treating “Southern Kurmanji” as a sharply bounded, homogeneous dialect.

## 2. Future and modal morphology: a documented contrast

Unger describes the Standard Kurmanji future construction as a modal particle (wê/dê/ê) with a present-stem verb carrying the subjunctive prefix bi-. The Badini future construction instead uses dê with a person-inflected present stem without bi-. The paper gives the Badini example:

- `vêca ez dê ç-ime malê` — “I will go home now.”

The exact transcription and segmentation above follow Unger’s example and gloss; they should not be silently normalised into a different orthographic standard.

Unger further reports that Badini dê cannot combine with the negation pattern used in the Standard Kurmanji future construction. The comparison presented in the paper is:
- Standard Kurmanji: `Ez-ê sibe bi wan re neçim` — “I won’t go with them tomorrow.”
- Behdinî equivalent: `Ez sibe digel wan naçim`.

This is an important warning: even where the English meaning is equivalent, the morphology and construction are not necessarily equivalent.

## 3. The Badini da construction

Unger documents a Badini modal particle `da` which, like dê, follows the subject and combines with a person-inflected present stem without modal/aspectual prefixes. Unlike dê, it does not express future time. The paper reports two major uses:
1. counterfactual eventualities in the present or near past;
2. habitual states of affairs in the distant past.

The article's examples include `ew da genimî çînin` (“[In old time] they used to plant wheat”) and other narrative examples. Unger explicitly notes that the distant-past interpretation is based on a relatively small number of corpus examples and that temporal distance may require further evidence. This limitation must accompany any pedagogical generalisation.

Unger argues that the Badini da construction carries functions that overlap with some Standard Kurmanji conditional forms, perfect subjunctive, and possibly second-future meanings. This is a functional comparison, not proof that the forms are morphologically identical or freely interchangeable.

## 4. Conditional paradigms: do not merge the systems

Unger reports that the Standard Kurmanji forms termed first conditional, second conditional, perfect subjunctive, and second future in Bedir Khan and Lescot are not present as the same paradigms in Badini; some of their functions are instead carried by the da construction.

The article's comparison includes:
- Standard Kurmanji first conditional: `Lezgîn dê bihata` (“Lezgin would have come”).
- Standard Kurmanji second conditional: `min-ê dîtî bûwa` (“I would have seen”).
- A perfect-subjunctive example with a past-stem subjunctive form.

These examples are reported as forms in the cited Standard Kurmanji descriptive tradition. They should not be presented as a complete productive Badini paradigm. Conversely, Badini da examples should not be inserted into a Standard Kurmanji paradigm as if they were alternative spellings.

Unger proposes a semantic explanation for the formal contrast, but explicitly notes that the account is not a complete proof and that differences of use may remain. The teaching documentation should therefore separate directly observed distribution from theoretical explanation.

## 5. Wider southeastern variation

Haig (2018) characterises Kurmanji as a dialect continuum and identifies Southeastern Kurmanji as one of its more differentiated endpoints; Behdinī of northern Iraq is included in this southeastern zone. The chapter notes comparatively rich morphology and more consistent retention of gender and ergativity in southeastern varieties.

Haig also states that Behdinī present subjunctive forms used for future tense regularly drop the prefix bi-. This independently corroborates the key morphological contrast reported by Unger. Haig notes that past-stem subjunctive endings show considerable cross-dialect variation, so a single set of past-subjunctive endings must not be extrapolated across the whole dialect continuum.

The regional classification is not a simple three-box taxonomy: Haig describes a northwest-to-southeast continuum, with blurred boundaries and mixed features in the intermediate Southern Kurmanji region. Therefore “Southern Kurmanji” should not be treated as a fully homogeneous or sharply delimited standard.

## 6. Implications for Avasin Academy Pathway B

### Recommended editorial policy (provisional)

1. Keep the core sequence anchored in one declared written reference norm: Standard Kurmanjî in Hawar-based Latin orthography, provisionally Cizîr/Botan-oriented.
2. Add a dialect note only when the form is directly attested and its locality/variety is identified.
3. Label Behdinî forms explicitly as Behdinî / Southeastern Kurmanji; do not mix them with the core paradigm in the same answer key.
4. For future and counterfactual meanings, distinguish:
   - Standard Kurmanji wê/dê/ê + subjunctive morphology;
   - Behdinî dê + present stem without bi-;
   - Behdinî da + present-stem construction for counterfactual and distant-past habitual readings.
5. Mark the Unger semantic account as an analysis/hypothesis, not as an unquestionable classroom rule.
6. Do not produce new paradigms by analogy. Require an attested source example or a reviewed native-speaker paradigm before adding a form.
7. Keep pronunciation and audio assessment out of scope until the project owner approves a target spoken variety and the recordings are reviewed.

### What this audit does not decide

- It does not select Behdinî as the Pathway B target.
- It does not claim every Duhok/Badinan locality uses identical forms.
- It does not establish a complete Behdinî tense-aspect-mood grammar.
- It does not authorise edits to existing lessons, course inventory, seed content, application code, CI, migrations, production data, PR #3, or deployment.
- It does not replace review by qualified native Kurmanji and Behdinî editors.
