## 1. Purpose and scope

This document narrows the previous B2–B3 grammar matrix to one difficult B3 learning problem: Kurmanji verbs do not all derive their present and past stems through one predictable operation. Learners and editors need a reliable way to record the principal parts of frequent verbs, distinguish stem alternation from inflectional endings, and avoid building false universal rules from a handful of regular examples.

The document covers:

- the two-stem description (present stem and past stem);
- a source-attested inventory of frequent verb stems;
- stem alternation patterns and lexical exceptions;
- the separation of stem, TAM prefix, person/number ending and clause alignment;
- an evidence-led method for selecting a small B3 teaching set;
- dialect and orthographic controls.

It does **not** prescribe a final Kurmanji house terminology, approve the proposed Cizre/Botan-leaning written baseline, certify a CEFR level, or authorize edits to existing lessons. The project inventory remains Course 1 = 26 lessons and Course 2 = 12 lessons.

## 2. Main finding

Haig (2018, §2.6) describes the typical Western Iranian pattern of two verbal stems in Kurmanji: a present stem and a past stem. The relation between the two is **not fully predictable**, although recurring patterns exist. His Table 12 lists frequent verbs with infinitive, past stem, present stem and meaning.

This has three consequences for course design:

1. Do not teach one mechanical rule such as “remove the infinitive ending to get the present stem” as if it applied to all verbs.
2. Record both stems as lexical information for high-frequency verbs.
3. Teach finite forms only after separating the stem from prefixes and agreement endings.

The label “irregular” should be used cautiously. A pair can be non-transparent to a learner without being historically irregular in every theoretical analysis. In the internal inventory below, **non-predictable / lexical stem pair** is the safer working description.

## 4. What the stem inventory does and does not establish

The inventory establishes that:

- some verbs have very short present stems (`bûn → b-`, `kirin → k-`, `dan → d-`);
- some pairs show substantial lexical alternation (`gotin → got- / bêj-`, `dîtin → dît- / bîn-`, `çûn → çû- / ç- or her-`);
- some verbs have similar or identical-looking stem shapes (`mirin → mir- / mir-`, `girîn → girî- / girî-`);
- some entries contain alternative stems that should not be collapsed without a variety label.

It does **not** establish that every listed alternative is interchangeable in every dialect, register, person form or construction. Nor does it prove that all verbs should be taught as “irregular”. A specialist should classify the alternations before a learner-facing rule is written.

## 5. Stem versus finite verb form

A useful internal representation is:

`(NEGATION) + (TAM / MOOD PREFIX) + STEM + (PERSON / NUMBER ENDING)`

This is a schematic teaching aid, not a complete linear template for every Kurmanji construction. Light-verb combinations, imperatives, preverbal elements and dialect-specific forms may differ.

For the selected reference variety, the editor should annotate every paradigm in four layers:

1. **Lexeme / infinitive:** the dictionary citation form.
2. **Stem:** present or past stem, with source.
3. **Prefix / TAM:** e.g. indicative, subjunctive, negation or past progressive marking, only where the source analysis supports it.
4. **Agreement ending:** person/number morphology, with its controller identified by clause type.

Do not label `di-` itself as “the present tense” in every context. Haig’s description distinguishes the present-stem forms, the different TAM prefixes, and past-stem forms with their own aspectual and negation patterns. Gündoğdu (2015) likewise analyses the interaction of negation, aspect/mood prefixes and stem selection as a morphological ordering problem.

## 6. Special cases that deserve separate notes

### 6.1 `zanîn` and `karîn`: negative present

Haig (2018) notes that `zanîn` (“know”) and `karîn` (“be able”) use `ni-` in the negative present, rather than the more general negative pattern described for many other verbs. This is a lexical exception worth recording in a teacher reference.

Do not overgeneralize this to all verbs beginning with a particular sound or ending. The form must be learned with the lexeme.

### 6.2 `hatin` and `anîn`: present forms

Thackston (2006) describes `hatin` (“come”) and `anîn` (“bring”) as having irregular present conjugations. The present forms are not adequately taught by simply attaching the regular present prefix and person endings to a stem guessed from the infinitive.

Because the exact paradigm and transcription differ across descriptions, the course should not copy a partial table from a secondary excerpt. Build the complete six-person paradigm from the chosen reference grammar and validate it with a native Kurmanji editor.

### 6.3 `dîtin`, `xwestin`, `gotin`

Thackston specifically highlights the present stems `bîn-` for `dîtin` and `xwaz-` for `xwestin`. Haig’s inventory additionally shows `bêj-` for `gotin`. These are strong candidates for a first high-frequency stem-pair teaching set, subject to source spelling and reference-variety review.

## 7. Recommended B3 selection strategy

Do not expose all 25 entries as one memorization wall. Build a compact, frequency- and contrast-based sequence:

| Teaching set | Candidate verbs | Reason for selection | Required review |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — first non-transparent pairs | `gotin`, `dîtin`, `xwestin`, `hatin` | High utility and clearly distinct present/past stems or special present forms | Full paradigms; confirm source and variety |
| B — short present stems | `bûn`, `kirin`, `dan`, `birin` | Prevent learners from assuming infinitive minus ending is always the stem | Validate segmentation and examples |
| C — recurring consonant alternations | `girtin`, `ketin`, `kuştin`, `avêtin` | Show that alternation is patterned but not reducible to one universal rule | Check phonological explanation and exact spelling |
| D — same/near-same stem shapes | `mirin`, `girîn`, `barîn` | Contrast verbs where the two stems are similar or identical in the cited inventory | Check all finite forms and aspect interpretation |
| E — lexical and regional extensions | `xistin`, `jîn/jîyan`, `hêlan/hiştin`, `çûn` | Entries with alternatives or additional constructional complexity | Require explicit dialect/lexical notes |

This is a proposed teaching order, not an approved vocabulary list. Confirm frequency against the course corpus or a representative contemporary Kurmanji corpus before final selection.

## 8. Minimum paradigm evidence required for each taught verb

Before a verb is admitted to a learner-facing B3 unit, its record should contain:

- source and page/table/section;
- selected infinitive and orthographic form;
- present stem and past stem;
- present indicative forms for all six persons;
- past intransitive forms where applicable;
- past transitive forms with an overt object, where applicable;
- negative forms;
- subjunctive/imperative only if in the lesson outcomes;
- a natural example sentence with translation and a stated variety;
- segmentation that distinguishes prefix, stem and ending;
- agreement controller in each clause type;
- native-editor confirmation of naturalness and spelling.

Do not infer an unlisted form by analogy if the verb is known to be irregular. Mark the missing form as **not yet verified**.

## 9. Dialect and orthography controls

1. Keep Standard Kurmanjî, Western Kurmanji, Behdînî and other regional forms distinct in evidence notes.
2. Never merge alternative stems into a slash-separated “free variation” label unless a source explicitly supports interchangeability.
3. Preserve diacritics and source transcription in the evidence record; any normalized house spelling must be recorded as a separate editorial decision.
4. Avoid treating a stem alternative as an orthographic variant unless the source demonstrates that it is only spelling.
5. Separate spoken-variation notes from the proposed written standard.
6. Do not import Sorani morphology or Sorani learner explanations into the fully Kurmanji-medium pathway without an independent Kurmanji analysis.
