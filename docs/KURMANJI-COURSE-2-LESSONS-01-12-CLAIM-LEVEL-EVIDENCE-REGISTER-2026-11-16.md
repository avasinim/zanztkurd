## 1. Evidence method and reference variety

This register expands the existing `KURMANJI-COURSE-2-12-LESSON-SOURCE-REVIEW-2026-11-16.md` into explicit claims. It does not replace the source review or the integrated 38-lesson action register.

Statuses:
- **SUPPORTED** — directly supported by a relevant source for the stated variety/construction.
- **PARTLY SUPPORTED** — the general proposition is supported, but exact examples, scope, or transcription need verification.
- **UNRESOLVED** — evidence reviewed does not settle the exact claim/example.
- **EDITORIAL** — Sorani wording, pedagogy, title, or assessment design requires owner/native-editor decision.

**Reference-variety caution:** Haig (2018) presents a grammatical sketch of “Standard Kurmanjî” loosely based on Cizre and Botan in southeastern Turkey, then separately discusses regional variation. It is not a claim that every Kurmanji-speaking community uses every paradigm identically. Haig notes that some western varieties show loss or merger of gender and ergativity features, while southeastern varieties retain richer morphology. Therefore, every generalization below is scoped to Standard Kurmanji unless a specific variety is named.

## 2. Lesson 01 — morphology and word structure

### C2-L01-01 — Morphology studies the internal structure of words and meaningful units
**Status: SUPPORTED AT GENERAL LINGUISTIC LEVEL**

The lesson's distinction between a word and a morpheme is a standard introductory morphological distinction. Haig (2018, §§2.2–2.6) demonstrates that Kurmanji grammatical information is distributed across bound nominal and verbal morphology.

**Editorial consequence:** retain the conceptual definition, but do not imply that all word segments are morphemes.

### C2-L01-02 — A morpheme is not the same thing as a phoneme or syllable
**Status: SUPPORTED AT GENERAL LINGUISTIC LEVEL**

The distinction is methodologically sound: phonemes are sound contrasts, syllables are prosodic units, and morphemes are form-meaning units. The lesson's warning not to identify every sound segment as a morpheme is important.

**Editorial consequence:** provide a minimal, Kurmanji-based worked segmentation rather than only abstract definitions.

### C2-L01-03 — `-ek` is a singular indefinite suffix in Standard Kurmanji
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.2.2) describes `-ek` as the singular indefinite marker and gives forms such as `gund-ek`. Its subsequent case/ezafe morphology depends on the construction.

**Editorial consequence:** use `gund-ek` as a worked example, but identify the root and suffix explicitly and do not confuse following case/ezafe endings with `-ek`.

### C2-L01-04 — Existing lesson examples fully demonstrate root, stem, derivation and inflection
**Status: PARTLY SUPPORTED / PEDAGOGICAL GAP**

The lesson explains the categories but provides too little Kurmanji segmentation to demonstrate all of them. The source review identifies `-ek` as the principal concrete example.

**Editorial consequence:** add examples only after specialist verification. Do not invent a derivational prefix example to fill a table.

## 3. Lesson 02 — root, stem, prefix and suffix

### C2-L02-01 — Root and stem are analytically distinct
**Status: SUPPORTED AT GENERAL LINGUISTIC LEVEL**

The distinction is standard morphological terminology. Kurmanji's verb system, with present and past stems and multiple affixes, makes the distinction pedagogically relevant (Haig 2018, §2.6; Gündoğdu 2015).

**Editorial consequence:** define root as the lexical core and stem as the form to which inflectional material attaches, while avoiding an over-rigid segmentation where the source does not establish the boundary.

### C2-L02-02 — Prefixes precede a stem and suffixes follow it
**Status: SUPPORTED**

This is a positional definition. Kurmanji uses both prefixes and suffixes, including TAM and negation prefixes and person/number suffixes (Haig 2018, §2.6; Gündoğdu 2015).

**Editorial consequence:** do not imply Kurmanji morphology is only suffixal.

### C2-L02-03 — The lesson's `-ek` example demonstrates a suffix
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.2.2) supports the indefinite suffix analysis.

**Editorial consequence:** retain, but add at least one verified verbal prefix example elsewhere in the lesson sequence to show both positions.

### C2-L02-04 — A fully worked Kurmanji prefix/root/suffix segmentation is already present
**Status: UNRESOLVED / PEDAGOGICAL GAP**

The source review found no fully worked prefix example in the lesson body. A possible analysis of `di-bêj-im` is plausible in the Standard Kurmanji present indicative, but exact segmentation and stem terminology should be checked against Haig's verb table before publication.

**Editorial consequence:** keep `di-bêj-im` as a candidate for specialist review, not as an already certified example.

## 4. Lesson 03 — noun, grammatical gender and number

### C2-L03-01 — Standard Kurmanji nouns have grammatical masculine/feminine gender
**Status: SUPPORTED WITH VARIETY SCOPE**

Haig (2018, §2.2.1) describes masculine and feminine grammatical gender and its relevance to nominal morphology and agreement. Haig & Öpengin (2018) and Haig (2018, §3) document regional reduction or loss of gender distinctions in some varieties.

**Editorial consequence:** explicitly label the paradigm Standard Kurmanji; do not say every regional dialect maintains the same gender system.

### C2-L03-02 — Grammatical gender is not equivalent to natural/biological sex
**Status: SUPPORTED AS A LINGUISTIC DISTINCTION**

Grammatical gender is a noun-class/agreement category. The lesson's distinction is conceptually correct.

**Editorial consequence:** keep the explanation brief and illustrate with nouns, not only human referents.

### C2-L03-03 — Gender is relevant to singular oblique marking and ezafe
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §§2.2.2 and 2.3.1) describes gender-sensitive singular oblique forms and ezafe agreement with the head noun.

**Editorial consequence:** teach gender, case and ezafe as related but distinct operations.

### C2-L03-04 — Standard Kurmanji direct plural is generally unmarked, while oblique plural commonly has `-a(n)`
**Status: SUPPORTED WITH SCOPE**

Haig (2018, §2.2.2, pp. 115–116) describes the direct plural as generally unmarked and the oblique plural as commonly marked by `-a(n)`. The distribution is not a universal rule for every variety or every lexical item.

**Editorial consequence:** the lesson currently mentions plural variation without teaching the core paradigm. Add a sourced paradigm and label its scope.

### C2-L03-05 — Direct indefinite plural `-in` is a universal Kurmanji plural marker
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED; DO NOT TEACH AS UNIVERSAL**

Haig (2018, §2.2.2) describes `-in` as restricted/variable rather than an across-the-board direct plural marker.

**Editorial consequence:** if included, label its distribution and source; do not present it as the general plural suffix.

### C2-L03-06 — Gender distinctions are neutralized in plural
**Status: SUPPORTED FOR THE STANDARD PARADIGM**

Haig (2018, §2.2.2) describes the plural system and neutralization of gender distinctions.

**Editorial consequence:** include this only alongside the actual direct/oblique plural examples so the learner can see what is and is not marked.

## 5. Lesson 04 — definite/indefinite nouns and `-ek`

### C2-L04-01 — `-ek` marks singular indefiniteness
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.2.2) gives `gund-ek` and other indefinite singular forms.

**Editorial consequence:** retain the core rule.

### C2-L04-02 — A bare noun may be definite or generic depending on context
**Status: SUPPORTED WITH CONTEXT**

The lesson's contextual account is appropriate; definiteness is not always encoded by a dedicated overt suffix.

**Editorial consequence:** avoid a categorical rule that every bare noun is definite. Explain the role of discourse and context.

### C2-L04-03 — `gund-ek-î` and `jin-ek-ê` illustrate indefinite plus oblique marking
**Status: SUPPORTED FOR STANDARD KURMANJI**

Haig (2018, §2.2.2) gives gender-sensitive singular oblique paradigms, and Haig & Öpengin (2015) corroborate feminine `-ê` and masculine `-î` in the standard paradigm. Dialectal forms differ.

**Editorial consequence:** show morpheme boundaries and the syntactic environment that licenses oblique case.

### C2-L04-04 — The ending `-î` has only one grammatical function
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED**

The same written form can be an oblique ending in one construction and masculine ezafe in another. Haig (2018, §2.3.1) gives `bajar-ek-î mezin` as a noun + indefinite + ezafe + adjective sequence.

**Editorial consequence:** preserve the lesson's useful distinction; add a side-by-side segmentation.

## 6. Lesson 05 — ezafe and noun–adjective relation

### C2-L05-01 — Adjectives follow the head noun and are linked by ezafe
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.3.1) gives `bajar-ek-î mezin` ‘a big town’ and describes the ezafe marker on the head noun.

**Editorial consequence:** retain the example after confirming exact transcription and punctuation.

### C2-L05-02 — Ezafe agrees with the head noun in gender and number in Standard Kurmanji
**Status: SUPPORTED WITH VARIETY SCOPE**

Haig (2018, §2.3.1) explicitly states that Standard Kurmanji ezafe inflects for gender and number and agrees with its head noun. Other dialects may differ.

**Editorial consequence:** say “in Standard Kurmanji” and do not teach the agreement paradigm as universal.

### C2-L05-03 — The adjective itself agrees in the same way as the ezafe
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED FOR THE STANDARD EXAMPLE**

In `bajar-ek-î mezin`, the adjective `mezin` does not change for the masculine gender of `bajar`; agreement is carried by ezafe.

**Editorial consequence:** keep the distinction explicit: ezafe agrees with the head; the adjective form in this example is invariant.

### C2-L05-04 — Possessors and adjectives follow the head noun and are linked by ezafe
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.3) describes the noun phrase sequence and shows possessors and descriptive adjectives after the head noun, linked by ezafe.

**Editorial consequence:** add a noun phrase with both possessor and adjective only after checking its exact morphology and ordering.

### C2-L05-05 — Linking ezafe and free/demonstrative ezafe are identical in distribution
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.3.1) distinguishes simple linking ezafe constructions from free/demonstrative ezafe forms. They are related historically and formally, but their functions and distributions differ.

**Editorial consequence:** preserve this distinction for the advanced follow-up in Course 1 lessons 21–24 and Course 2.

## 8. Lesson 07 — personal pronouns and direct/oblique case

### C2-L07-01 — Kurmanji has direct and oblique personal-pronoun forms
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.2.3, Table 9) gives the pairs `ez/min`, `tu/te`, `ew/wî,wê`, `em/me`, `hûn/we`, `ew/wan`.

**Editorial consequence:** retain the paradigm, with the standard-variety scope.

### C2-L07-02 — Direct is equivalent to subject and oblique is equivalent to object
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED**

The distribution depends on tense/aspect and transitivity. The oblique form may mark a logical subject in canonical past transitive clauses; direct forms may occur as objects in that alignment.

**Editorial consequence:** explicitly warn against translating case labels into fixed grammatical roles.

### C2-L07-03 — Past transitive clauses use oblique subject and direct object in the canonical pattern
**Status: SUPPORTED FOR STANDARD KURMANJI; DIALECTAL EXCEPTIONS EXIST**

Haig (2018, §2.7.2), Haig & Öpengin (2018), and Baker & Atlamaz (2014) describe the split alignment. Baker & Atlamaz document double-oblique constructions in Muş/Serhed varieties.

**Editorial consequence:** include a standard example and label the dialect exception; do not claim all Kurmanji varieties share one agreement pattern.

### C2-L07-04 — Past transitive agreement is with the direct object in the canonical pattern
**Status: SUPPORTED WITH VARIETY SCOPE**

Baker & Atlamaz (2014) directly analyse object agreement in canonical past transitive clauses; their Muş data also show default third-singular agreement in double-oblique clauses.

**Editorial consequence:** state the standard/canonical pattern, not an exceptionless universal.

## 9. Lesson 08 — verb, stem, person and number

### C2-L08-01 — Kurmanji verbs have distinct present and past stems
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.6.1) and Gündoğdu (2015) describe present and past stem forms as central to the verbal paradigm.

**Editorial consequence:** retain the distinction and avoid assuming one invariant lexical stem with simple tense suffixes.

### C2-L08-02 — Person/number agreement is expressed by verbal suffixes
**Status: SUPPORTED WITH TAM/ALIGNMENT CONDITIONS**

Gündoğdu (2015) describes person-number markers in suffix positions. The argument controlling agreement depends on tense and transitivity; canonical past transitive clauses differ from present transitive and intransitive clauses.

**Editorial consequence:** teach agreement only after identifying tense/aspect, transitivity and case alignment.

### C2-L08-03 — The same agreement marker always indexes the grammatical subject
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED**

In canonical past transitive clauses, agreement indexes the direct object, not the oblique agent. In certain dialects, double-oblique constructions show default third-singular agreement.

**Editorial consequence:** avoid a subject-only agreement rule.

### C2-L08-04 — The lesson contains sufficient worked verb paradigms and morpheme segmentation
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED BY CURRENT PEDAGOGICAL COVERAGE**

The source review found that the lesson states the principle but provides few actual paradigms or segmentations.

**Editorial consequence:** add a compact, source-checked contrast set, e.g. present transitive, past intransitive, and past transitive, only after exact transcription and segmentation are verified.

## 11. Lesson 10 — negation and questions

### C2-L10-01 — Kurmanji negation is morphologically sensitive to stem/TAM structure
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Gündoğdu (2015) analyses negation within a templatic verbal morphology. The chapter describes distinct present and past stems and the interaction of negation with TAM prefixes.

**Editorial consequence:** do not teach all negation as one invariant prefix attached to any verb form.

### C2-L10-02 — The negation markers include `n(a)-`, `n(e)-`, and `ni-`
**Status: SUPPORTED, WITH CONSTRUCTIONAL DISTRIBUTION**

Gündoğdu (2015, chapter abstract and full chapter text) identifies three morphological markers, `n(a)-`, `n(e)-`, and `ni-`, and discusses their distribution and blocking interactions. Haig (2018, §2.6.1) documents relevant standard examples and regional patterns.

**Editorial consequence:** present the forms in a paradigm with tense/TAM and variety labels; do not reduce them to a simplistic one-form-per-tense rule.

### C2-L10-03 — Negation competes with TAM prefixes in the verbal template
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Gündoğdu (2015) argues that negation and TAM prefixes may occupy the same slot and that their co-occurrence is restricted by blocking effects. The exact configuration differs between present and past stems: negation may block a present-stem TAM prefix, while past progressive can retain `di-` alongside negation `ne-`.

**Editorial consequence:** a teaching table must distinguish present-stem and past-stem behavior. Do not claim negation always blocks every TAM marker.

### C2-L10-04 — `nizanim` and `nikarim` are relevant exceptional forms
**Status: SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §2.6.1) and Gündoğdu (2015) discuss `ni-` forms such as `nizanim` and `nikarim`.

**Editorial consequence:** preserve the forms with their proper lexical and variety context; do not overgeneralize `ni-` beyond attested environments.

### C2-L10-05 — Standard and western-dialect past progressive negation are identical
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED**

Haig (2018, §3.4) documents variation in western varieties, including differences in negation and verbal morphology. A single standard pattern should not be applied to all western Kurmanji.

**Editorial consequence:** label regional forms; do not present the standard paradigm as pan-dialectal.

### C2-L10-06 — The lesson's nominal-clause negation explanation is fully demonstrated
**Status: NOT SUPPORTED BY CURRENT LESSON BODY**

The reviewed lesson introduces non-verbal/nominal-clause negation but does not provide enough worked Kurmanji examples and a clearly sourced variety label.

**Editorial consequence:** hold new examples until a source and native speaker confirm them; if using regional examples from Haig, mark the dialect explicitly.

### C2-L10-07 — The lesson's question-formation section contains source-verified Kurmanji examples
**Status: UNRESOLVED**

The source review found that the question section is largely a heading/explanation without worked question forms. The sources cited in the review establish general Kurmanji grammar but do not yet verify the exact intended yes/no and information-question examples in the lesson.

**Editorial consequence:** do not invent or add question sentences. Build a separate source list for question formation and obtain specialist review before insertion.
