## Source-backed details to restore or clarify

1. **The forecast about loss of the contrast (p. 418):** the source predicts that if current contact pressure continues, the aspiration contrast could disappear within a century, leaving aspirated forms at word beginnings and unaspirated or semi-aspirated forms medially/finally. The authored lesson says the contrast may weaken but omits this specific forecast and resulting distribution.
2. **Etymology of “hiatus” (p. 419):** the source gives the Latin origin and glosses it as a gap, cleft or opening. The lesson defines the phonological phenomenon but omits this etymological note.
3. **Vowel-combination table details (pp. 420–422):** the lesson summarizes the main combinations but does not retain several source examples and qualifications, including *naelimînin*, *saet/itaet*, *Haîtî*, *aort*, *automat/autonom*, *meaş/seat*, *seet*, *meîde*, *neon*, *iade/idia*, *dienirin*, *diore*, *îane* and *îon*. The source's hyphens mean that no example was found in that cell, not that the sequence is categorically impossible. The last printed table page (422) repeats some row labels in the extracted text; do not infer or silently normalize unclear vowel-pair labels without inspecting the page image.
4. **The “linking Y” terminology (p. 424):** the source explicitly corrects the common label “tîpa kelijandinê”: Y is not the letter that joins hiatus, but the letter used to prevent hiatus. The lesson notes Y's role but does not retain this explicit terminological correction.
5. **Third-person singular suffix note (p. 425, footnote 316):** the source identifies the suffix *-e* in *dijî + e* as third-person singular. The authored example is present but the footnote explanation is absent.
6. **Alternative consonant insertions (p. 427):** the source gives *du-y-em, du-w-em* and *amade-t-î* as alternatives to H in derived forms. The lesson names Y/W/T but omits these concrete forms.
7. **T variation (p. 428):** the source gives *heval + î → hevaltî* and also the alternate *hevalînî*. The latter is absent.
8. **Movable N and plural examples (pp. 428–429):** the lesson explains written *-an* versus spoken *-a* and the use of N before a following vowel, but omits the source examples *kurdan, kitêban, bajaran* versus *kurda, kitêba, bajara*, and the phrases *ez du kurda dibînim* / *ew dostê kurdan e*.
9. **Historical/typological note for movable N (p. 429):** the source identifies the best-known parallel as Ancient Greek and cites the term movable/euphonic N. The lesson mentions the term and Turkish *o → ona* but omits the Ancient Greek comparison.
10. **Poetry and word-boundary hiatus (p. 430):** the source adds that the spoken boundary process also appears frequently in poetry. The authored lesson describes spoken Eastern Kurmanji but omits poetry.

## Existing source inconsistencies preserved

- The printed example *dîsa, dîsa* on p. 428 is duplicated; p. 429 clearly gives *dîsa/dîsan*. The lesson records this instead of inventing a correction.
- The printed form *neyînîn* on p. 426 differs from the later discussion's *neînin* and *neyne*. The lesson flags the discrepancy rather than silently normalizing it.
- The final vowel-combination table page (p. 422) has repeated row labels in the extracted text. Its unclear labels must not be reconstructed by guesswork.
