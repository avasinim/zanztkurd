# Kurmanji Preverbs, Particle Verbs, Incorporation and Inflectional Prefix Placement — Evidence Audit — 2026-11-17

## 1. Scope

This audit distinguishes lexical preverbs, inflectional prefixes, incorporated compounds, separate noun/adjective + light-verb predicates and directional clitics. The central finding is dialect-sensitive: Standard Kurmanji commonly places inflection between a preverb and verbal stem, while Şemzînan (SEK) can place inflection before a lexicalized preverb+stem complex. These are not interchangeable orders.

Documentation only: no lessons, course inventory, code, seed, schema, migration, CI, database, production, PR or deployment is changed.

## 2. Terms and method

A **preverb** is a lexical element such as **ra-**, **hil-**, **da-**, **ve-** or **der-** that combines with a verbal stem. An **inflectional prefix** such as indicative/imperfective **di-**, negation **na-/ne-**, or subjunctive **bi-** expresses grammatical information. An **incorporated preverb** forms part of a lexicalized stem in the relevant variety; a **non-incorporated preverb** remains outside the inflectional stem. A **directional -e** is a post-verbal clitic and is not a preverb.

Writing an infinitive as one word does not prove that the preverb is synchronically incorporated: inflected forms may reveal an internal boundary.

## 3. Standard Kurmanji: inflection between preverb and stem

Haig & Öpengin (2018, §3.4.1.2) describe opaque preverbal particles such as **hil**, **řā/ra** and **da** combining with stems to form new verbs. In the infinitive they are usually written together. In Standard K., inflectional prefixes such as negation and indicative/imperfective are inserted between preverb and stem.

Example:
- infinitive **raketin** ‘go to sleep’;
- Standard K. **ra-di-keve** ‘goes to sleep’.

Gündoğdu (2016) likewise gives:
- **der-na-kev-e** ‘he/she does not go out’;
- **der-di-kev-e** ‘he/she is going out’.

Here **der-** contributes lexical direction/result, while **na-/di-** marks grammatical polarity/aspect on the verbal element. These examples do not establish a complete paradigm for every preverb or verb class; stem allomorphy and agreement must be checked separately.

## 4. Dialect contrast: incorporation in Şemzînan

Haig & Öpengin report that in Şemzînan (SEK), imperfective and negative prefixes often precede the preverbal element. This ordering indicates lexicalization of preverb + stem as a new stem in that variety.

Their example for ‘rise’:
- SEK **di-helē-t**, analysed as a contraction of **di-hel-hē-t**;
- Standard K. counterpart **hil-t-ê**.

The negative SEK form likewise places grammatical prefixes before the preverb. This is a dialect-specific morphological contrast, not a free stylistic alternative to Standard K.

Editorial decision: label every example by variety. Do not combine Standard K. and SEK forms into one paradigm or teach them as interchangeable without a target-variety decision.

## 5. Preverb compounds versus separate N/ADJ + light-verb predicates

Gündoğdu (2015) treats Kurmanji compound verbs as evidence for templatic morphology. The analysis distinguishes preverb + light-verb formations such as **vekirin**, **hildan**, **raketin** from separate non-verbal element + light-verb predicates such as **alîkarî kirin**.

For the latter, inflection appears on the verbal element:
- **alîkarî dikin** — affirmative;
- **alîkarî nakin** — negative.

For preverb compounds, inflection can be inserted between preverb and verb, e.g. **der-na-kev-e** and **der-di-kev-e**. Thus:
1. preverb + verb compounds have internal prefix placement in the described Standard K. pattern;
2. separate N/ADJ + light-verb predicates inflect the verbal element;
3. exceptional lexicalized constructions may show a different placement.

Orthographic joining alone cannot decide whether a non-verbal element is an object, complement or incorporated preverb. Argument structure needs its own analysis.

## 6. Exceptional left-edge negation

Haig & Öpengin note that in some highly lexicalized and frequent light-verb constructions, negation may occur at the left edge of the entire verbal complex, before the non-verbal element. Example:

**min ne=ber-ē xū da-yē** — ‘I did not look at him/her/it.’

This is a construction-specific exception, not a general rule allowing negation before every noun, adjective or preverb. Record the whole predicate, preserve source segmentation, label it lexicalized, and do not extend the pattern to unrelated N+V combinations.

## 7. Negation and TAM: blocking

Gündoğdu (2015), “Negation in Kurmanji,” argues that affix ordering is templatic. The chapter identifies main negative prefix shapes **n(a)-**, **n(e)-** and **ni-**, with distribution conditioned by TAM and stem type. In relevant prefix slots, negation blocks affirmative prefixes such as progressive/indicative **di-** and subjunctive **bi-**. Therefore one must not mechanically stack **na- + di-** or **ne- + bi-** as if each occupied an independent slot.

Haig & Öpengin (2018) give dialect-sensitive qualifications:
- Standard Kurmanji distinguishes negative indicative and subjunctive patterns;
- Badini has no dedicated negative future in the described pattern and can use negative indicative instead;
- western varieties can differ in the negative prefix used with past imperfective forms;
- imperative negation may have its own marker.

These are inflectional facts, separate from the lexical ordering of preverbs. Do not transfer one dialect's negative forms to another.

## 8. Directional -e is not a preverb

Haig & Öpengin describe directional **-e** (often [æ]) after the person marker on many motion or directed-action verbs (‘go’, ‘come’, ‘give’, ‘speak’, ‘send’). It is analysed as a reduced directional preposition cliticized to the verb.

It differs from lexical preverbs:
- preverbs occur before the verbal stem and participate in lexical verb formation;
- directional **-e** follows the person marker and contributes directional/goal information;
- its placement and function must not be inferred from surface adjacency alone.

## 9. Diagnostic procedure for lexical records

For every candidate:
1. Record the infinitive exactly, including joined/separate spelling.
2. Identify the verbal stem and stem allomorphs.
3. Obtain affirmative and negative inflected examples.
4. Locate **di-**, **na-/ne-** or **bi-** relative to the preverb.
5. Label the variety (Standard K., SEK, Badini, western Kurmanji, or a named local variety).
6. Decide whether the preverb is incorporated, non-incorporated or unresolved.
7. Separate lexical preverbs from directional **-e**.
8. For N/ADJ + light verbs, record where inflection occurs and whether left-edge negation is independently attested.
9. Check each TAM category rather than extrapolating from one form.
10. Keep orthographic convention separate from morphological analysis.

## 11. Academy decisions

- Preserve the Standard K. pattern **preverb + inflection + verbal stem** when supported by the target source.
- Keep SEK prefix-before-preverb ordering as a separately labelled dialect contrast.
- Distinguish preverb compounds from separate N/ADJ + light-verb predicates.
- Treat left-edge negation in highly lexicalized predicates as construction-specific.
- Keep directional **-e** separate from lexical preverbs.
- Teach negation/TAM blocking; do not stack competing prefixes mechanically.
- Do not derive prefix placement from infinitive spelling alone.
- Do not edit existing lessons until the target standard, dialect labels and example paradigms receive review and explicit approval.

## 13. Conclusion

Kurmanji preverb placement depends on lexicalization and dialect. Standard Kurmanji commonly inserts inflection between preverb and stem; Şemzînan can place it before a lexicalized preverb+stem complex. Separate N+V predicates have their own inflectional behavior, and only narrowly attested lexicalized predicates show left-edge negation. Directional **-e** is a distinct post-verbal clitic. Negation and TAM interact through blocking in the affix template.

**Decision:** maintain separate, variety-labelled paradigms and lexical records. Do not present dialectally different orders as interchangeable.
